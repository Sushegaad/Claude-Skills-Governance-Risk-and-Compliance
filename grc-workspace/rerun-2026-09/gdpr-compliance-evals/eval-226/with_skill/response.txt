Short answer: No — as designed, this is very likely unlawful under Article 22 GDPR, and it sits squarely within the fact pattern that just produced the largest Article 22 fine on record.

Rule (Art. 22)
Article 22(1) gives data subjects the right "not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing... which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly affects him or her." Deactivating a driver's account is a textbook "similarly significant effect" — it removes their ability to earn income, which courts and regulators treat as at least as serious as many "legal effects." A fraud-detection model that flags an account and triggers deactivation with no human in the loop is "solely automated" for Art. 22 purposes unless a human reviews the individual case and has genuine discretion to overturn it — a human merely rubber-stamping the model's output does not count as meaningful review.

Controlling precedent: Uber (Dutch AP, August 21, 2026)
This scenario is functionally identical to the case that produced the second-largest GDPR fine ever: €824.99 million against Uber, issued by the Dutch Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (lead supervisory authority under one-stop-shop, with CNIL cooperating after a complaint from 171 French drivers via Ligue des droits de l'Homme). The AP found that between 2018 and 2022, Uber fully automatically deactivated driver accounts — temporarily on fraud suspicion or low ratings, permanently on persistent low ratings — with no human assessment, and gave drivers insufficient information about the logic and consequences of the automated decision. The AP's deputy chair stated plainly: "A computer should not make decisions on its own that have major consequences for you. These decisions should have been looked at first by a human being." Uber has appealed, but the decision is now the defining enforcement precedent for Art. 22 in platform/gig-economy contexts.

Exceptions (Art. 22(2)) and why they likely don't help here
Solely automated decisions are permitted only if: (a) necessary for entering/performing a contract, (b) authorised by EU/Member State law with suitable safeguards, or (c) based on explicit consent. Even where an exception applies, Art. 22(3) still requires the controller to implement "suitable measures to safeguard the data subject's rights and freedoms and legitimate interests, at least the right to obtain human intervention... to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision." A blanket contractual clause permitting automated deactivation would not satisfy this — you still owe drivers a genuine contestation channel.

Practical implications / what to change
1. Human review must be real: a qualified reviewer must be able to examine the individual case, access the underlying signals, and overturn the flag — not merely confirm the model's output within a fixed SLA.
2. Transparency (Art. 13/14, Art. 15(1)(h)): drivers must be told that automated decision-making is used, given meaningful information about the logic involved, and told the significance and likely consequences.
3. Contestation channel (Art. 22(3)): provide an accessible, timely route to challenge a deactivation before or immediately after it takes effect, especially where account suspension causes immediate loss of income.
4. DPIA (Art. 35): profiling that produces legal/significant effects at scale is a mandatory DPIA trigger — document necessity, proportionality, and mitigations, and consult your DPO.
5. Audit the fraud model itself for accuracy and bias, and log every automated decision and any human override for accountability (Art. 5(2)).
