Scraping publicly available web data for AI training is one of the most contested areas of GDPR enforcement right now, and "it's public" is not a defense — GDPR applies to personal data regardless of whether it was previously made public by the data subject or a third party. Several DPAs (notably the Italian Garante's actions against Clearview AI and, temporarily, against OpenAI/ChatGPT in 2023, plus ongoing EDPB and CNIL guidance on AI and web scraping) have made clear that scraping the public web for training data falls squarely within GDPR's scope whenever the data includes information relating to identified or identifiable natural persons.

Here's how to think through it:

1. Lawful basis. You need one under Article 6 before you scrape anything containing personal data. In practice, for large-scale scraping, the two realistic options are:
   - Legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)): the most commonly invoked basis for AI training. This requires a documented Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA) balancing your interest in training the model against the rights and reasonable expectations of the individuals whose data appears in the scraped content. The EDPB's 2024 Opinion 28/2024 (issued in response to the Irish DPC's request concerning AI models) sets out a structured three-step test: (a) identify a legitimate interest, (b) show the processing is necessary for that purpose, (c) balance against the data subject's rights, considering factors like the reasonable expectations of individuals (would someone posting a forum comment reasonably expect it to train a generative model?), the nature of the data (sensitive/special category data scraped incidentally is a serious red flag), and the availability of mitigations.
   - Consent is rarely feasible at web-scraping scale, so most organizations don't rely on it, though for narrow, targeted scraping it could apply.
   Note there is no "public data" exemption in GDPR — Article 9's special-category rules explicitly still apply even to data "manifestly made public by the data subject," it just changes the analysis (Article 9(2)(e)) rather than removing it.

2. Special category data. Web scrapes routinely pick up special category data (health, religion, sexual orientation, political opinion, trade union membership, etc.) incidentally, e.g., from social media bios, forum posts, or personal blogs. Processing this requires an Article 9(2) condition; "manifestly made public" is one option but is interpreted narrowly and requires clear evidence the individual intended broad public disclosure, not just that content was technically accessible.

3. Transparency (Articles 13/14). Because you didn't collect the data directly from the data subjects, Article 14 applies, including the exemption at Article 14(5)(b) where informing individuals "proves impossible or would involve disproportionate effort" — which is the argument most large AI developers rely on for bulk scraping, but you still must take proportionate steps: publish clear, accessible information about training data sources and purposes, and this exemption is being tested and narrowed by DPAs, not a guaranteed shield.

4. Data minimisation and purpose limitation. Scraping indiscriminately without any filtering for personal data increases risk. Practical mitigations regulators look favorably on: filtering/excluding sites known to contain high volumes of sensitive personal data, excluding data behind privacy walls or robots.txt/ToS restrictions, de-duplication, removing or masking direct identifiers where feasible without destroying training utility, and respecting opt-out signals (e.g., "noai" tags, Do Not Train registries some platforms now support).

5. Data subject rights. You must have a practical mechanism for individuals to exercise rights — access, erasure, objection (Article 21, which has particular force for legitimate-interest processing since data subjects have an unconditional right to object to processing for certain purposes, and you must be able to honor deletion/objection even though data may already be embedded in trained model weights, which is a genuinely hard technical and legal problem regulators are actively grappling with — see ongoing debate about whether "unlearning" or model retraining is required).

6. International transfers and jurisdiction. If any scraped content includes EU residents' data and it's processed/trained outside the EEA, standard cross-border transfer rules (SCCs, adequacy, etc.) still apply.

7. DPIA. Given the scale, novel technology, profiling potential, and use of special-category and possibly children's data, a DPIA under Article 35 is very likely mandatory before you begin.

Bottom line: it can be done in a GDPR-compatible way, but "publicly available" does not mean "GDPR-free." You need a documented legitimate interest assessment, filtering to reduce special-category and sensitive data ingestion, a transparency mechanism (even if relying on the disproportionate-effort exemption), a working rights-request process, and likely a DPIA — and you should expect continued regulatory scrutiny and evolving guidance in this area, so build in flexibility to adjust your scraping and training pipeline as EDPB/DPA positions solidify.
