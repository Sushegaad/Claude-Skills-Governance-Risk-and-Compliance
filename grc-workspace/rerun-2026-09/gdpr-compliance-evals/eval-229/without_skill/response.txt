Here are the key clauses your Data Processing Agreement (DPA) with the hosting provider needs to include, mapped to the mandatory content requirements of Article 28(3) GDPR, plus a few practical additions worth negotiating.

1. Subject matter, duration, nature, and purpose of processing
State precisely what is being processed (EU customer personal data — specify categories: e.g., names, contact details, account data, any special categories if applicable), the duration of the agreement, and the specific purpose (hosting infrastructure/storage services for [Company]'s platform). Vague or boilerplate descriptions are a common audit finding — be specific.

2. Types of personal data and categories of data subjects
List them explicitly (e.g., customer names, email addresses, IP addresses, transaction data; data subjects = your customers/end users). This should be maintained as a living annex/schedule so it can be updated without renegotiating the whole agreement.

3. Processing only on documented instructions (Article 28(3)(a))
The processor must process personal data only on your documented instructions, including regarding international transfers, unless required by EU/member state law — in which case it must inform you before processing (unless prohibited from doing so on public interest grounds).

4. Confidentiality commitment (Article 28(3)(b))
All persons authorized to process the data must be bound by confidentiality obligations (contractual or statutory).

5. Security measures (Article 28(3)(c), tied to Article 32)
Require appropriate technical and organizational measures reflecting the state of the art, costs, and risk — specifically: encryption at rest and in transit, access controls and authentication, logging/monitoring, regular testing/evaluation of measures, and resilience/backup and disaster recovery capability. Attach a security annex with concrete specifics (not just "industry standard security") since hosting providers in particular should commit to specifics like data center physical security, patching cadence, and incident detection capability.

6. Sub-processor provisions (Article 28(2) and 28(3)(d))
- General or specific prior written authorization for engaging sub-processors (cloud infrastructure providers commonly use general authorization with a notification mechanism).
- A right for you to object to new sub-processors within a defined notice period (commonly 15-30 days), with a clear consequence if you object (e.g., right to terminate the affected service or the agreement).
- A flow-down obligation: the same data protection obligations must be imposed on sub-processors via contract, and the processor remains fully liable to you for sub-processor failures.
- A current, accessible list of sub-processors (often maintained as a public or portal-based register).

7. Assistance with data subject rights (Article 28(3)(e))
The processor must assist you, taking into account the nature of processing, in responding to data subject requests (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection) — typically via technical/organizational measures and defined response timeframes (e.g., processor notifies you within 48-72 hours of receiving a request directly).

8. Assistance with Article 32-36 compliance (Article 28(3)(f))
Processor must help you meet obligations around security, breach notification, DPIAs, and prior consultation with supervisory authorities, taking into account the information available to the processor.

9. Personal data breach notification (feeds Article 33/34)
This is critical for a hosting provider specifically — require notification to you without undue delay, and specify a firm outer limit (commonly 24-72 hours from becoming aware), including what information must be provided (nature of breach, categories/approximate number of data subjects and records affected, likely consequences, measures taken/proposed). Since your ultimate 72-hour clock to the supervisory authority starts when you become aware, the processor's notification speed is your critical path — negotiate the shortest commercially reasonable window.

10. End-of-contract data return/deletion (Article 28(3)(g))
Processor must, at your choice, delete or return all personal data at the end of the provision of services, and delete existing copies unless EU/member state law requires storage — including from backups, on a defined schedule.

11. Audit and inspection rights (Article 28(3)(h))
Processor must make available all information necessary to demonstrate compliance and allow for/contribute to audits, including inspections, conducted by you or an auditor you mandate. Hosting providers often substitute independent third-party audit reports/certifications (SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001) for direct audits — acceptable, but negotiate a fallback right to a full audit for cause (e.g., after a breach).

12. International transfers
If the hosting provider processes or stores data outside the EEA/adequate countries (common with global hosting infrastructure — check specific data center locations, not just company HQ), include Standard Contractual Clauses (2021 EU Commission modules, processor-to-processor or controller-to-processor as applicable), plus require a Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) addressing government access risks in the destination country (post-Schrems II requirement), supplementary measures (encryption with keys held by you, not the processor), and a commitment to notify you of government access requests where legally permitted.

13. Liability and indemnification
Allocate liability for GDPR fines and third-party claims arising from processor non-compliance; ensure this isn't capped below a level that reflects realistic GDPR exposure (fines up to 4% of global turnover).

14. Data localization/segregation options
Consider requiring EU-only data residency for EU customer data specifically, and clarify whether data is logically or physically segregated from other tenants.

Practical tip: don't just accept the hosting provider's standard "Data Processing Addendum" — many are drafted processor-favorably (e.g., broad general sub-processor authorization with weak objection rights, breach notification "without undue delay" with no outer time limit, liability caps that don't cover regulatory fines). Redline these specific points before signing.
