Direct answer: Under Article 28(3) GDPR, any contract with a processor handling personal data on your behalf must contain a specific, mandatory set of clauses — a hosting provider storing EU customer data is a textbook processor relationship, and missing any of these terms is itself a compliance violation exposed to Art. 83(4) fines (up to €10M or 2% of global turnover). Below are the key clauses to include, gathered before drafting: your organisation's role (controller), the processor's identity, the categories of data/data subjects, and — critically — where the hosting infrastructure is physically located, since that determines whether you also need an international transfer clause.

1. Subject Matter, Duration, Nature and Purpose (Art. 28(3))
State precisely what the processor does: "[Processor] shall process personal data solely for the purpose of providing hosting and infrastructure services for [Controller]'s application, for the duration of the underlying services agreement dated [DATE]." Vague or open-ended purpose language is a common audit finding.

2. Categories of Data and Data Subjects (Art. 28(3))
List explicitly: e.g., contact data (name, email), account credentials, usage/log data (IP addresses, session identifiers), and confirm whether any special category data (Art. 9) is in scope — hosting providers often handle this without either party flagging it.

3. Processing Only on Documented Instructions (Art. 28(3)(a))
"Processor shall process personal data only on documented instructions from Controller, including regarding international transfers, unless required by applicable law — in which case Processor shall inform Controller before processing, unless legally prohibited from doing so."

4. Confidentiality (Art. 28(3)(b))
Personnel authorized to access the data must be bound by confidentiality obligations, contractual or statutory.

5. Security Measures (Art. 28(3)(c); Art. 32)
Specify concrete technical and organisational measures: encryption at rest and in transit, access controls, ability to restore availability/access after an incident, and regular testing/evaluation of security measures. For a hosting provider specifically, insist on details of physical security, network segmentation, and backup encryption — generic "industry standard security" language is insufficient for audit purposes.

6. Sub-processors (Art. 28(2), (3)(d), (4))
Require prior specific or general written authorisation before engaging sub-processors (e.g., a CDN, backup provider, or cloud region operator the hosting provider itself relies on), with a right to object to new sub-processors, a current sub-processor list (as a schedule), and flow-down of equivalent obligations — the hosting provider remains liable to you for its sub-processors' failures.

7. Assistance with Data Subject Rights (Art. 28(3)(e))
The provider must assist you, via appropriate technical/organisational measures, in responding to access, erasure, rectification, and other Art. 15–22 requests — important for hosting since backups and infrastructure-level deletion capability are frequent gaps (flagged as a top failure mode in the EDPB's 2025 erasure enforcement sweep).

8. Assistance with Controller Obligations (Art. 28(3)(f))
Assistance with Art. 32 security, Art. 33–34 breach notification, and Art. 35–36 DPIAs, proportionate to the nature of processing and information available to the provider.

9. Deletion or Return of Data (Art. 28(3)(g))
On termination, the provider must delete or return all personal data (your choice) and delete existing copies, including backups, unless law requires retention — specify a concrete deletion timeline (e.g., 30/60 days) rather than leaving it open-ended.

10. Audit Rights (Art. 28(3)(h))
Right to audit or inspect the provider's compliance, or accept independent certifications/reports (e.g., SOC 2, ISO 27001) in lieu of a full audit, with a defined notice period.

11. Breach Notification Timeline (Art. 33)
Require notification "without undue delay" and within a specific window (commonly 24–48 hours) of the provider becoming aware of a breach, so you retain enough runway to meet your own 72-hour Art. 33 deadline to the supervisory authority.

12. International Transfers (Art. 44–49) — confirm before finalizing
If the hosting infrastructure or any sub-processor/backup region is outside the EEA/UK, you need a transfer mechanism clause: an adequacy decision (Art. 45 — e.g., UK adequacy renewed through 27 December 2031), 2021 Standard Contractual Clauses (Art. 46(2)(c) — still the current version), or Binding Corporate Rules (Art. 47). For US-based hosting relying on the EU–US Data Privacy Framework, note it remains valid but is under CJEU appeal (Case C-703/25 P) with PCLOB oversight currently suspended — build in SCCs as a contractual fallback rather than relying on DPF alone.

⚠️ Legal Advice Disclaimer: This guidance is informational and does not constitute legal advice. Have final contract language reviewed by a qualified data protection lawyer or your DPO, particularly the security specifications, sub-processor authorisation mechanism, and international transfer clause once the provider's actual data locations and sub-processors are confirmed.
