I'm sorry you're dealing with this — ransomware with possible exfiltration of 40,000 EU customers' personal data is a serious incident, and the clock is already running. Here's a step-by-step walkthrough of your GDPR obligations, roughly in the order you need to act.

Immediate (hours 0-24): Containment and initial assessment
- Your incident response/security team should already be isolating affected systems, but from a compliance standpoint, the priority is establishing facts: what data was involved, whether exfiltration is confirmed or merely possible, how many data subjects and what categories of data (special category data like health or financial details raises severity significantly), and when the incident actually began versus when you became "aware" of it.
- "Awareness" is a defined legal trigger — per EDPB guidance and case law (including the 2023 CJEU clarification in Case C-340/21), the 72-hour clock under Article 33 starts when you have a reasonable degree of certainty a breach has occurred, not necessarily when you have full details. Document precisely when your team first had that reasonable certainty — this timestamp will be scrutinized.
- Engage your DPO (or equivalent) immediately if you have one; if not, get legal/privacy counsel involved now, since notification decisions carry legal risk either way.

Within 72 hours: Notify the supervisory authority (Article 33)
- You must notify the lead supervisory authority (typically where your main EU establishment is, or where the most affected data subjects are, if you lack an EU establishment) within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to individuals' rights and freedoms — which, given ransomware and possible exfiltration of 40,000 records, is very unlikely to apply here. Assume notification is required.
- If you can't gather all details in time, Article 33(4) allows phased/staged notification — submit what you know within 72 hours and supplement "without undue further delay."
- Required content (Article 33(3)): nature of the breach including categories and approximate number of data subjects/records affected; name/contact of your DPO or other contact point; likely consequences of the breach; measures taken or proposed to address it and mitigate adverse effects.
- If you're late past 72 hours, Article 33(1) requires you to include reasons for the delay — document your timeline and rationale contemporaneously, don't reconstruct it later.
- Multi-jurisdiction note: if affected customers span multiple EU member states, use the one-stop-shop mechanism via your lead authority, but be prepared for cooperation with other national DPAs.

Notify affected individuals (Article 34) — assess in parallel
- Required "without undue delay" if the breach is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. Ransomware plus exfiltration of customer personal data is a common trigger for high risk (identity theft, fraud, phishing exposure), so build the presumption that individual notification is needed unless you can demonstrate otherwise.
- Exceptions to direct notification (Article 34(3)): you had implemented appropriate protection (e.g., strong encryption that renders the data unintelligible to the attacker) making the data unintelligible; you've since taken measures ensuring the high risk is no longer likely to materialize; or direct notification would involve disproportionate effort, in which case a public communication or similar measure is required instead.
- Content of individual notice should be in clear, plain language: nature of the breach, likely consequences, measures taken/recommended (e.g., password resets, fraud monitoring, watch for phishing), and your contact point.

Internal documentation (Article 33(5)) — do this regardless of notification decision
- Document every breach internally: facts, effects, and remedial action taken — this record must be available for the supervisory authority to verify compliance, even for breaches you determine don't require notification. This documentation obligation is absolute and often overlooked.

Broader obligations to work through concurrently
- Article 32 security review: assess whether your technical and organizational measures were "appropriate" to the risk — the incident itself will likely be treated by the regulator as evidence one way or the other, so be prepared to show what safeguards existed (encryption, access controls, backups, patching cadence) and what you're remediating.
- Processor/controller chain: if the affected systems or data involve a processor (e.g., a vendor holding the data on your behalf) or if you are a processor for another controller, you have parallel/derivative notification obligations — processors must notify controllers without undue delay under Article 33(2), and if you're a processor, notify your controller immediately so they can meet their own 72-hour clock.
- Law enforcement and other regulators: ransomware often triggers separate obligations (e.g., NIS2 for essential/important entities, national cybercrime reporting, sector regulators, potentially SEC-style disclosure if you're a public company with US exposure, and law enforcement engagement, especially if considering whether to pay a ransom, which carries its own legal/sanctions risk).
- Post-incident: expect the supervisory authority may open an investigation, request further information, or conduct an audit. Preserve all forensic evidence, incident response records, and your decision-making rationale (the "why" behind your risk assessment and notification timing) — this becomes essential in demonstrating compliance and mitigating potential fines under Article 83, where cooperation and remediation are aggravating/mitigating factors.

Practical next steps right now: confirm your "awareness" timestamp, get counsel and DPO involved today, begin drafting the Article 33 notification even before all facts are final, and run the Article 34 high-risk assessment in parallel so you're not making that call under time pressure at hour 70.
