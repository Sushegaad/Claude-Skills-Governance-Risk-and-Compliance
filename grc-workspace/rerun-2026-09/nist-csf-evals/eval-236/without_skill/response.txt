Short answer: NIST itself does not publish a transit-specific version of the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), but there is a well-developed body of sector guidance you should layer on top of CSF 2.0, and several transit-focused resources that map directly to CSF functions and categories.

1. NIST CSF 2.0 as your foundation
CSF 2.0 (released February 2024) is deliberately sector-agnostic. It gives you six functions — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover — and expects each sector or company to build an "organizational profile" that tailors the subcategories to its own risk context. For a transit agency, that context includes both IT (fare systems, HR, finance, email) and OT/ICS (train control, signaling, SCADA for traction power, positive train control, transit signal priority, CCTV, fare gates, and increasingly connected/autonomous vehicle systems). CSF's Govern function is a good fit for transit because it forces explicit conversations about risk appetite, board/leadership oversight, and supply chain risk — all things transit agencies historically underinvested in relative to safety-critical operations.

2. Surface transportation / transit-specific guidance to combine with CSF
- TSA Security Directives and the TSA/DHS "Surface Transportation Cybersecurity Toolkit" — TSA has issued cybersecurity requirements for higher-risk surface transportation entities (originally aimed at freight rail and rail transit, with some extension to other modes) requiring designation of a cybersecurity coordinator, incident reporting to CISA, a cybersecurity implementation plan, and vulnerability assessments. These directives explicitly reference NIST CSF as an acceptable framework for structuring your program.
- APTA (American Public Transportation Association) has published cybersecurity guidance and recommended practices for transit systems, including a Control and Communications Security recommended practice and enterprise cybersecurity framework guidance for both rail and bus operations. APTA's work is generally structured to align with, or explicitly reference, the NIST CSF functions.
- FTA (Federal Transit Administration) has issued cybersecurity guidance and resources for transit agencies, including recommendations tied to the NIST CSF and to safety management systems (since FTA regulates transit safety via SMS/PTASP).
- CISA's Transportation Systems Sector guidance and the Sector-Specific/Sector Risk Management Agency materials for transportation include cyber-physical convergence guidance relevant to rail and bus signaling systems.
- NIST's own cross-sector guidance for industrial control systems — NIST SP 800-82 (Guide to Operational Technology Security) — is directly relevant to your rail signaling, SCADA, and traction power systems, and NIST SP 800-53 provides the control catalog you'd use if you need to get more granular than CSF subcategories.

3. Practical approach
- Treat CSF 2.0 as your governance and communication framework (useful for board reporting, budget justification, maturity tracking, and cross-department alignment between IT security, OT/rail engineering, and safety).
- Use TSA Security Directive requirements and CISA reporting obligations as your compliance floor — these are mandatory if you're a designated higher-risk agency.
- Use APTA and FTA recommended practices for transit-specific operational detail (e.g., segmentation guidance for train control networks, fare system PCI considerations, CCTV/access control convergence).
- Use SP 800-82 for OT-specific control selection within your Protect/Detect subcategories.
- Build a single organizational profile that explicitly separates IT and OT/safety-critical asset categories, since transit agencies typically have very different risk tolerances and change-management constraints between the two (a signaling system patch cadence is nothing like a corporate laptop patch cadence).

4. Governance note
Given TSA's mandatory security directives for transit, I'd recommend explicitly cross-walking your CSF profile subcategories to the TSA directive requirements (cybersecurity coordinator designation, incident reporting timelines, vulnerability assessment cadence, implementation plan content) so you have one profile that satisfies both your voluntary CSF maturity goals and your regulatory obligations. If you're not sure whether your agency is currently designated as "higher-risk" under the TSA directives, that's worth confirming with your general counsel or TSA point of contact, since it changes which requirements are mandatory versus best-practice.

In short: no CSF-transit crosswalk exists as an official NIST publication, but combining CSF 2.0 with TSA's surface transportation directives, APTA/FTA guidance, and NIST SP 800-82 for OT gives you a defensible, sector-appropriate program.
