Short answer: yes, generative AI can meaningfully accelerate a CSF assessment and profile-drafting process, and NIST has published relevant guidance — though not a single document titled "how to use GenAI for CSF assessments." You'll need to combine a few sources and apply sound judgment about where AI assistance is appropriate versus where it introduces risk.

1. Relevant NIST guidance
- NIST AI RMF 1.0 (AI Risk Management Framework) and its Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) are the primary references for how to think about risks introduced by using AI tools in your own operations, including risks like confabulation/hallucination, data leakage, and overreliance on automated output. If you use GenAI to help write your CSF profile, that use itself is an AI risk management question, and AI RMF's Govern/Map/Measure/Manage functions are a natural complement to CSF's own Govern function.
- CSF 2.0's Govern function (GV.OV, GV.RM, GV.SC, etc.) is exactly where AI-assisted tooling decisions belong — you should document in your governance records that GenAI tools are used to support (not replace) the assessment, who reviews the output, and what data is or isn't fed into the tool.
- NIST has also published guidance specifically on AI and cybersecurity risk (e.g., NIST IR drafts on adversarial machine learning, and CSF Quick-Start Guides that occasionally reference AI as an emerging risk area to consider under Identify's risk assessment category), but none of this constitutes a certified "use ChatGPT to fill out your CSF profile" methodology.

2. Where GenAI genuinely helps
- Drafting narrative text for organizational profile sections (mission statement framing, risk appetite language, subcategory implementation descriptions) based on interview notes or existing policy documents you feed in.
- Summarizing long policy/procedure documents to identify which CSF subcategories they already address, speeding up the "current profile" gap analysis.
- Generating a first-pass crosswalk between your existing controls (e.g., ISO 27001, SOC 2, CIS Controls) and CSF subcategories, which you then validate.
- Drafting interview questions and evidence-request lists for control owners.
- Turning raw assessment notes into a structured target profile document, tiering language (Tier 1–4), and gap-analysis tables.

3. Where you should not lean on GenAI unsupervised
- Actual risk ratings or maturity tier determinations — these require organizational judgment, actual evidence review, and accountability; an LLM has no visibility into your real environment and can confidently state something plausible but wrong.
- Feeding sensitive data (asset inventories, vulnerability scan results, incident details, network diagrams) into a public/consumer GenAI tool. If you use GenAI for this work, use an enterprise-tier tool with a data processing agreement and confirm your inputs aren't used for model training, or use an internally hosted model.
- Treating AI-generated subcategory implementation statements as automatically evidenced/true — every claim in your profile ("we implement PR.AC-1 via X") needs an actual control owner to confirm and ideally attach evidence.
- Final sign-off — the CSF profile is a governance artifact; leadership and control owners need to review and approve the substance, not just skim AI output.

4. Practical workflow recommendation
- Use GenAI as a drafting and summarization assistant, not a decision-maker.
- Keep a human-in-the-loop review step for every subcategory before it's marked "implemented" in your profile.
- Log which parts of the assessment used AI assistance, consistent with your organization's AI governance policy (this is itself a good CSF GV.SC / GV.PO practice to document).
- If your organization already has an internal generative AI acceptable-use policy or an AI governance committee, route this use case through it, since assessment documents can contain sensitive risk information.
- Consider building a lightweight internal "prompt library" for common CSF assessment tasks (e.g., "summarize this policy against CSF subcategory X.X and flag gaps") so results are consistent across assessors and reviewers.

In short: NIST's AI RMF and Generative AI Profile are the closest things to official guidance on using AI tools responsibly, and CSF 2.0's Govern function is where you document how you're using them. There's no NIST-endorsed "AI-run CSF assessment" methodology — treat GenAI as an efficiency tool for drafting and synthesis, with humans owning every judgment call and sign-off.
