Here's a practical organizational profile approach for a mid-size manufacturer adopting CSF 2.0. CSF 2.0 formalizes "organizational profiles" as the core mechanism for applying the framework to your specific business — you'll build a Current Profile (where you are today) and a Target Profile (where you want to be), then use the gap between them to drive your roadmap.

1. Scope and business context (feeds Govern function, GV.OC)
Start by defining organizational context: what you make, your supply chain structure (tier 1/2/3 suppliers, OEM relationships), regulatory drivers (e.g., CMMC/DFARS if you're a defense subcontractor, ITAR, state privacy laws, customer security requirements in contracts), and your risk tolerance. Manufacturers typically have a bimodal environment: corporate IT (ERP, email, engineering CAD/PLM systems) and OT/ICS on the plant floor (PLCs, HMIs, SCADA, MES). Your profile should explicitly note this split because Protect/Detect/Respond controls differ substantially between the two (e.g., patching a PLC isn't the same cadence as patching a laptop, and safety systems may have hard real-time constraints that limit certain security controls).

2. Select a scoping unit
Decide whether your profile covers the whole enterprise or is built per-plant/per-business-unit. For a mid-size manufacturer with a handful of plants, I'd recommend one enterprise-level profile for corporate IT and shared services, plus either a single OT profile that covers common plant-floor risk themes, or per-plant OT profiles if your plants have meaningfully different technology stacks or risk exposure (e.g., one plant handles export-controlled products, another doesn't).

3. Build the Current Profile
- For each of the six functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover), and each subcategory within, assess current state: implemented / partially implemented / not implemented / not applicable.
- Gather evidence from existing documentation: IT security policies, OT network diagrams, vendor risk questionnaires you've already answered for customers, past audit/pen-test results, incident history, insurance questionnaires (cyber insurance applications are a surprisingly good existing gap-analysis input).
- Interview control owners: IT security lead, plant/OT engineering lead, HR (for insider risk/onboarding-offboarding), procurement (supply chain risk), and a business continuity/safety lead (Recover function, tied into existing business continuity/disaster recovery plans you likely already have for safety/operations reasons).
- Score using CSF Tiers (1–4: Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) at the organizational level, and implementation status at the subcategory level. Don't conflate the two — tiers describe how mature and integrated your risk management practices are overall, not a per-subcategory status.

4. Build the Target Profile
- Prioritize based on actual business drivers: customer contractual requirements (increasingly common for manufacturers supplying to larger OEMs or the defense industrial base), insurance requirements, known incident history, and realistic budget/headcount.
- Be selective. A common mistake is targeting Tier 4/"fully implemented" across the board — that's rarely necessary or affordable. Set differentiated targets: e.g., higher rigor for Identify/Protect subcategories touching OT and safety systems or IP-sensitive engineering data, moderate rigor for general corporate IT, lower rigor for low-risk peripheral systems.
- Pay special attention to Govern (new emphasis area in 2.0): supply chain risk management (GV.SC) is particularly relevant for manufacturers given third-party component risk, contract manufacturers, and software supply chain (PLC firmware, MES software vendors).

5. Gap analysis and roadmap
- For each subcategory where Current < Target, document the specific gap, the remediation action, an owner, and a realistic timeline (quarters, not just "someday").
- Group gap-closure actions into a 12–24 month roadmap, sequencing quick wins (policy documentation, MFA rollout on remaining accounts) ahead of larger capital/OT projects (network segmentation between IT and OT, SCADA monitoring tooling), since OT changes often require plant downtime windows.
- Tie budget requests to specific target profile gaps — this framing (funded gap-to-target vs. abstract "cybersecurity spend") tends to work well with manufacturing leadership focused on capital planning cycles.

6. Governance and maintenance
- Assign an executive sponsor and a cross-functional review (IT security + OT/engineering + safety + legal/compliance) at least annually, or after significant events (new plant, acquisition, major incident, new customer contract with security requirements).
- Re-baseline the Current Profile periodically (annually is typical) and track Tier progression as a leadership-reportable metric.
- Use community/sector profiles where available as accelerators — NIST has published example profiles for some sectors, and manufacturing-specific resources (e.g., NIST's manufacturing profile work under the earlier CSF 1.1 era, and CISA/ISA guidance for ICS security) can inform your OT-specific target subcategory choices, even though you'll still need to tailor them.

Bottom line: build one enterprise Current/Target profile pair, explicitly split IT vs. OT considerations within it, prioritize targets by real business drivers rather than blanket maturity goals, and turn the gap into a funded, owned, dated roadmap reviewed at least annually.
