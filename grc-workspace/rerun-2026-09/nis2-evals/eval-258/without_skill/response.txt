Here's how a NIS2-in-scope entity should walk through incident reporting for a ransomware event that takes down production systems, based on the directive's Article 23 framework (note: exact procedural details, portals, and forms depend on your specific national transposition law, so treat this as the EU baseline to map against your country's implementation).

Step 1: Determine if it's a "significant incident." Not every security event triggers NIS2 reporting. A significant incident is one that (a) has caused or is capable of causing severe operational disruption or financial loss to the entity, or (b) has affected or is capable of affecting other natural or legal persons by causing considerable material or non-material damage. Ransomware taking down production systems almost always clears this bar — operational disruption is the textbook example, and if the ransomware also affects downstream customers, suppliers, or exposes personal data, the "affecting others" prong is triggered too. Don't spend time debating materiality once production is down; assume reportability and start the clock.

Step 2: The 24-hour early warning. Within 24 hours of becoming aware of the incident, you must submit an "early warning" to your national CSIRT or competent authority. This is deliberately lightweight — it should indicate whether the incident is suspected to be caused by unlawful or malicious action (ransomware clearly is), and whether it could have a cross-border impact (relevant if you operate in multiple member states). You will not have full facts at this stage — that's expected. The clock starts from when you "become aware," which is a defined trigger point, so make sure your incident response playbook has a clear internal escalation path that timestamps awareness (e.g., SOC detection, EDR alert, ransom note discovery) so you can demonstrate you met the deadline.

Step 3: The 72-hour incident notification. Within 72 hours, submit a fuller notification updating the early warning and providing an initial assessment: severity and impact, indicators of compromise if available, and (if known) the threat actor. For ransomware, this is where you'd typically include: which systems/production lines are affected, estimated downtime, whether data exfiltration occurred (increasingly common with double-extortion ransomware), and initial containment actions taken. If you don't have complete information yet, provide what you have and flag it as preliminary — NIS2 does not require certainty at this stage, only good-faith, timely disclosure.

Step 4: Intermediate reports (on request). The competent authority or CSIRT can request a status update at any point during the response if the incident is ongoing. Be prepared for this, particularly if the ransomware event drags on (recovery from backups, forensic investigation, negotiations if applicable).

Step 5: The final report. Within one month of the 72-hour notification (or one month after the incident is resolved if it's still ongoing at that point, with a progress report at the one-month mark and a final report once resolved), submit a final report containing: a detailed description of the incident including severity and impact; the type of threat or root cause that likely triggered it; mitigation measures applied and ongoing; and, where relevant, the cross-border impact of the incident.

Additional considerations specific to ransomware:

- Ransom payment: NIS2 itself doesn't regulate whether you can pay a ransom, but many member states have separate rules, sanctions screening obligations (the threat actor or wallet could be on an EU/OFAC sanctions list), and law enforcement notification expectations. Coordinate with legal counsel before any payment decision — this is a distinct workstream from the NIS2 reporting clock.
- Parallel obligations: Ransomware very often involves personal data compromise, which triggers GDPR Article 33/34 breach notification (72 hours to the supervisory authority, and to affected individuals if high risk) as a separate but overlapping obligation. Map your incident response so one factual timeline feeds both the NIS2 CSIRT notification and any DPA notification, to avoid inconsistent narratives.
- Customer/downstream notification: if the entity providing the service to you or that you provide services to is affected, the competent authority can require or you may need to voluntarily inform affected recipients of your service, particularly where a significant cyber threat is identified that could materially affect them.
- Voluntary reporting: Even for incidents that don't clearly meet the "significant" threshold, NIS2 encourages voluntary notification of near-misses, other cyber threats, and incidents affecting entities outside strict scope — worth knowing since regulators view a mature voluntary-reporting culture favorably.
- Documentation: Because "awareness" timestamps and the good-faith/preliminary nature of early submissions matter for defensibility, maintain a clean incident timeline log from first detection through final report, ideally reviewed by legal/compliance before submission at each stage.

Practical recommendation: Build (or confirm you already have) an incident response runbook that hard-codes these three clocks (24h/72h/1 month) with named owners for each report, a pre-approved communication template, and a decision tree for parallel GDPR/DPA notification, sanctions screening (if ransom payment is considered), and customer notification — so that during an actual live ransomware event your team is executing a rehearsed plan rather than interpreting the regulation for the first time under pressure.
