I can't tell you definitively whether you're an "essential" or "important" entity without knowing your sector, size, and specific role in your national economy — that classification is fact-specific and ultimately confirmed by your competent authority or self-assessed against the criteria in the transposed national law. What I can do is give you the classification test to run and explain why the distinction matters in practice.

How the essential/important split works under NIS2:

First, sector test. NIS2 splits covered sectors into two annexes. Annex I lists "sectors of high criticality": energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, wastewater, digital infrastructure (cloud, data centers, DNS, TLD registries, trust services, public electronic communications), ICT service management (managed service providers, managed security service providers), public administration, and space. Annex II lists "other critical sectors": postal/courier services, waste management, chemicals manufacturing/distribution, food production/processing/distribution, manufacturing (medical devices, computers/electronics, machinery, motor vehicles), digital providers (online marketplaces, search engines, social networking platforms), and research organisations.

Second, size test (the general rule). Within Annex I sectors, medium and large entities (50+ employees or €10M+ annual turnover/balance sheet) are essential entities. Within Annex II sectors, medium and large entities are important entities. Small and micro entities are generally out of scope entirely, unless they meet special criteria below.

Third, the exceptions that override the size test. Regardless of size, an entity is automatically essential if it is: the sole provider of a service critical for maintenance of vital societal/economic activities in a member state; identified as critical under the separate CER Directive (Critical Entities Resilience); a public administration entity of central government (or, at member state discretion, regional level); a qualified trust service provider or TLD registry/DNS provider (these are essential regardless of size); or an entity whose disruption could have significant systemic effects or cross-border impact. Member states can also designate specific entities as essential or important individually, and can lower the size thresholds for particular sectors if justified by risk.

Fourth, some Annex I entities are essential purely by category regardless of size test outcome — for example DNS service providers, TLD registries, and qualified trust service providers are always essential; while Annex I entities not fitting these auto-categories fall to essential (if medium/large) via the general size rule.

Why the distinction matters practically:

1. Supervisory regime — this is the biggest practical difference. Essential entities are subject to proactive, ex-ante supervision: competent authorities can conduct regular audits, on-site inspections, security scans, and request information even without a prior incident or complaint. Important entities are subject to reactive, ex-post supervision only — the authority generally only investigates after evidence of a problem (an incident, a complaint, a tip) comes to light. This is a materially lighter supervisory burden for important entities in day-to-day terms.

2. Fines — essential entities face administrative fines up to €10,000,000 or 2% of total global annual turnover (whichever is higher); important entities face up to €7,000,000 or 1.4% of global turnover (whichever is higher). Both are calibrated to be dissuasive, but the essential-entity ceiling is notably higher.

3. Registration and identification — essential entities are more likely to be individually identified/registered by the competent authority (with obligations to provide extensive information), whereas important entities in many national schemes self-identify and self-register within a set timeframe after the law's entry into force.

4. Substantive obligations are the same either way — this is the part people often get wrong. Article 21 risk-management measures (the ten-point list: risk analysis, incident handling, business continuity, supply chain security, security in acquisition/development, effectiveness assessment, cyber hygiene/training, cryptography, HR/access control/asset management, and use of MFA/secure communications) and Article 23 incident reporting obligations (24h/72h/1-month) apply identically to both essential and important entities. So "important" does not mean "less compliance work" — it means "less proactive government scrutiny," not "fewer requirements to meet."
