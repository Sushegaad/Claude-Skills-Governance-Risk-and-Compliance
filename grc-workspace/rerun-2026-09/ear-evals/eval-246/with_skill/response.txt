Classification and Export Path — Commercial Surveillance Drone (2.5-Hour Endurance) to Brazil

**Step 1 — Jurisdiction and ECCN.** Applying the mandatory Order of Review: first confirm the UAV is not enumerated or "specially designed" for military use under the USML (22 CFR Part 121) — a purely commercial surveillance drone typically is not. That places it under EAR jurisdiction. Under the current drone classification framework (final rule, 91 FR 52501, effective August 13, 2026), endurance is the threshold test: at 2.5 hours, your drone falls below the 3-hour line and classifies as **9A012.a.1**, controlled **AT-only** (the NS2 basis was removed in this rule). This is a materially more permissive classification than the pre-2026 regime.

Software and technology follow the hardware: NS was also removed from **9D001, 9D002, 9D004, and 9E001** for sub-3-hour UAVs, so associated flight-control software, ground-station software, and technical data for this airframe are likewise AT-only, assuming no other controlled subsystems are involved.

**Step 2 — Check for controlled subsystems (override condition).** Before relying on the AT-only classification, verify the payload. If the drone incorporates a **6A003 thermal imager**, a **6A005 laser** (e.g., laser rangefinder/illuminator), or **7A001–7A005** navigation-grade components (e.g., high-grade IMU or GPS-denied nav system), the platform reverts to **NS1/AT1** regardless of endurance. Surveillance payloads are the most common trigger here — confirm the sensor package's technical specifications against these ECCNs before finalizing classification.

**Step 3 — Country Chart analysis for Brazil.** Assuming the AT-only classification holds (no NS-triggering subsystems), check the Commerce Country Chart AT Column 1 for Brazil. Brazil is not an embargoed destination, not on the E:1/E:2 lists, and AT Column 1 restrictions apply almost exclusively to state sponsors of terrorism — Brazil does not fall in that category, so no license is required on AT grounds (**NLR — No License Required**).

**Step 4 — End-user and end-use controls still apply.** A "no license required" ECCN outcome does not end the analysis. Under § 744.21, military end-use/end-user controls now expressly reach ECCNs 9A012, 9D001, 9D002, 9D004, and 9E001 — screen the Brazilian distributor and its downstream end-users against the Entity List, Denied Persons List, Unverified List, and Military End-User List via the Consolidated Screening List. If the distributor or an identified end-user has a military or intelligence-service nexus, a license may be required even though the Country Chart shows no "X."

**Step 5 — Distributor due diligence.** Because this is a distributor relationship (resale to unknown downstream end-users), obtain a signed distributor agreement with EAR compliance flow-down clauses, an end-user statement or reasonable assurance of civil end-use, and screen for red flags under § 732.6 (reluctance to identify end-users, unusual payment routing, requests to obscure the ultimate destination). Re-screen the distributor at least monthly per standard ECP practice.

**Step 6 — Documentation and recordkeeping.** Retain the ECCN classification rationale (endurance data, payload technical specs, confirmation no 6A003/6A005/7A001-005 components are present), the Country Chart lookup, screening records, and the distributor agreement for 5 years from export (§ 762.6). File EEI/AES if the shipment value exceeds $2,500 (Part 758).

**Bottom line:** With no NS-triggering payload, this drone is 9A012.a.1 (AT-only) and generally exportable to Brazil without a BIS license, but Entity List/MEU screening of the distributor and end-user is mandatory, and the classification should be documented and revisited if the sensor payload changes. If any ambiguity remains about payload classification, consider a CCATS request via SNAP-R for a binding ECCN determination before shipment.

*This is general compliance guidance, not legal advice — verify current Country Chart entries and consult export counsel before shipping.*
