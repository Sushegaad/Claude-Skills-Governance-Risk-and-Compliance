Classification of a Military-Purposed UAV Not Described on the USML

**Step 1 — Confirm the jurisdiction call itself.** The Order of Review (§ 732.3) requires checking the USML first: if the UAV or its components are enumerated or "specially designed" for a defense article under 22 CFR Part 121, it is ITAR, full stop, regardless of any commercial-sounding marketing. Your premise — that it is a military-purposed UAV genuinely *not* described on the USML — assumes that test has been carefully worked through, including the "specially designed" catch-all analysis, which is often where classification disputes actually arise. If there is any real ambiguity (e.g., the airframe has both a civil variant and a military variant, or it was recently transferred out of USML jurisdiction under Export Control Reform), the safer move before finalizing the answer below is a **Commodity Jurisdiction (CJ) request to DDTC**, or a **CCATS request via SNAP-R** to lock in BIS's own ECCN determination in writing.

**Step 2 — Current classification: 9A012.a.** Assuming the jurisdiction call is settled and the item is confirmed EAR, BIS's current position (reversing its 2013 approach) is that "specially designed" military UAVs not described on the USML classify in the **600-series** as **9A012.a**. This is a deliberate policy shift: previously, many military-application UAVs outside USML enumeration defaulted to less specific 9A991/EAR99-adjacent treatment; BIS now captures them explicitly under the 600-series military-commodity structure, which carries the licensing rigor associated with 600-series items — presumption of denial to most destinations outside close allies, and mandatory military end-user screening.

**Step 3 — Less-sensitive subcomponents: 9A610.y.33.** Parts, components, and accessories of these military UAVs that are less sensitive — not the complete airframe or its most sensitive subsystems — classify separately under the newly created **9A610.y.33**. The ".y" paragraph structure in 600-series ECCNs typically signals a lower control tier (often subject to fewer license exceptions restrictions and sometimes de minimis relief), so correctly separating "the airframe/core system" (9A012.a or its 9A610-adjacent treatment) from ".y.33" components matters for your licensing burden — do not lump the entire bill of materials under the more restrictive parent entry without checking each component against the .y.33 description.

**Step 4 — STA availability.** Strategic Trade Authorization (License Exception STA) may be available for shipments to Country Group A:5 (the 42 allied/partner countries) — but only where the UAV **cannot deliver at least 500 kg of payload to at least 300 km range** (the MTCR Category I threshold). If your platform meets or exceeds that payload/range combination, STA is not available regardless of destination, and you should expect to need an actual BIS license with likely interagency (State/DoD) review.

**Step 5 — Military end-use/end-user controls apply.** Under § 744.21, military end-use/end-user controls now expressly cover ECCNs 9A012, 9D001, 9D002, 9D004, and 9E001. Screen every destination and end-user against the Military End-User (MEU) List and Entity List before proceeding — note also that the older "wind-gust parameter" test some practitioners historically used for UAV classification has been eliminated in the current framework and should not be relied upon.

**Recommendation:** (1) Confirm the USML/EAR jurisdiction line is correctly drawn — get it in writing via CJ/CCATS if there's any doubt; (2) classify the core airframe as 9A012.a and separately classify lower-sensitivity components under 9A610.y.33; (3) check payload/range against the 500 kg / 300 km MTCR Category I threshold to determine STA eligibility to A:5 destinations; (4) run § 744.21 military end-use/end-user screening on every transaction regardless of ECCN outcome; (5) document the full classification rationale and retain for 5 years (§ 762.6).

*General compliance guidance, not legal advice — the USML/EAR boundary for "specially designed" military articles is fact-intensive; consult export counsel or file a CJ/CCATS request for a binding determination.*
