Screening Lessons for a Chinese University Research Institute Buying Lab Neuroscience Equipment

This scenario sits directly on top of a live enforcement precedent, and the lesson from it should shape your entire screening approach rather than just a single checklist item.

**The governing lesson: the Plexon enforcement action (settled August 2026).** BIS settled with Plexon, Inc. for $1.7M (fully suspended over a 5-year probationary period, waived on completion of an external audit and a clean compliance record) over eight unlicensed exports (~$179K total value) of neural-recording systems to China's Academy of Military Medical Sciences — an entity that had been on the Entity List since 2021. The core lesson: **research and medical technology is not inherently exempt from Entity List screening.** A product being scientific, civilian-market, or apparently benign (neural recording amplifiers, electrophysiology equipment) does not substitute for actually screening the named institutional buyer. Apply that lesson directly here.

**Step 1 — Classify the equipment itself.** Determine whether your lab neuroscience equipment (recording amplifiers, electrode arrays, stimulation systems, associated software) falls on the CCL or is EAR99. Many neuroscience research instruments are EAR99, but some signal-processing, sensor, or associated software components may fall under Category 3 (electronics) or Category 5 (information security, if there's encrypted data transmission/storage). Document the classification rationale regardless of outcome — the Plexon case involved equipment that may not have carried an exotic ECCN; the violation was the *end-user*, not necessarily an exotic classification.

**Step 2 — Screen the named institute, not just "a Chinese university."** Run the specific institute's legal name, known aliases, and any parent/affiliate entities through the Consolidated Screening List (trade.gov/consolidated-screening-list), which covers the BIS Entity List, Denied Persons List, Unverified List, Military End-User List, and OFAC's SDN/Consolidated Sanctions Lists in one search. Many Chinese research institutions have military-affiliated parent organizations, PLA research ties, or are among the so-called "Seven Sons of National Defence" universities and their affiliates — a name that sounds like a purely academic research institute may sit under, or feed research directly into, an Entity-Listed military research organization. Check for shared addresses, shared leadership, or subsidiary relationships, not just an exact name match.

**Step 3 — Recognize that China is Country Group D:1, which narrows your options.** If any component of the equipment does carry an NS-controlled ECCN, note that CIV (Civil End-Users) is the primary exception theoretically available to D:1 destinations — but it requires a written end-user/end-use certification and is heavily scrutinized by BIS precisely because China is D:1. Do not treat CIV as a routine pass-through; treat it as a heightened-diligence exception.

**Step 4 — Apply § 744.21 military end-use/end-user controls and WMD/military-intelligence end-use checks (§ 744.6).** Even EAR99 equipment requires a license if the exporter knows or has reason to know of a prohibited military end-use or if the end-user is properly captured under military end-user controls. Ask direct questions about the specific research program the equipment will support, and document the answers.

**Step 5 — Watch the red flags from § 732.6.** In this fact pattern, red flags include: reluctance to specify the exact research application; requests for capabilities beyond typical academic research use; unusual or urgent payment terms; requests to ship through a freight forwarder or intermediate consignee rather than directly to the institute; or refusal to sign an end-user statement.

**Step 6 — Build the file before you ship, not after.** Obtain: a signed End-User Statement identifying the specific lab, principal investigator, and research purpose; documented Entity List/CSL screening results with date and screener name; the ECCN/EAR99 classification rationale; and internal sign-off from your Export Control Officer if any red flag was raised and resolved. Retain all of this for 5 years (§ 762.6) — the retention obligation applies whether or not you conclude a license is required.

**Bottom line:** Treat "Chinese university research institute" as a name requiring full-depth Entity List and affiliate screening before quoting the order, not after. The Plexon case shows BIS will pursue penalties even where the exporter may have believed the transaction was ordinary academic/medical trade — the compliance failure was inadequate screening of a listed military-research end-user, not necessarily a classification error.

*General compliance guidance, not legal advice — consult export counsel, particularly given the elevated scrutiny BIS applies to China D:1 research-institution transactions.*
