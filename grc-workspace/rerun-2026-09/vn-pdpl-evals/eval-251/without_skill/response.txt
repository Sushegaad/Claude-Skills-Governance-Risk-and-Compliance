Vietnam's personal data protection regime has moved from decree-level rules (Decree 13/2023/ND-CP on Personal Data Protection) toward a full Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL), which the National Assembly has been advancing with an effective date around 2026. As of late 2026, the enforcement landscape blends the original Decree 13 penalty framework with sharper, law-backed sanctions that regulators (the Ministry of Public Security's Cybersecurity and Data Protection department, A05/A06) have signaled they will use more aggressively.

Administrative penalties: Under Decree 13 and its implementing sanctions decree (the amendments to Decree 15/2020 on ICT administrative penalties, updated via Decree 14/2022 and further revisions), fines for personal data violations have historically been capped in the hundreds of millions of VND per violation (roughly 100 million–500 million VND, i.e., approximately USD 4,000–20,000 per breach type). However, the PDPL introduces a notable shift long discussed in draft versions: revenue-based fines for serious or repeated violations, potentially up to 5% of a violating company's total revenue in the preceding fiscal year in Vietnam — mirroring the GDPR's turnover-based approach. This is the single biggest change data protection officers need to understand: penalties are no longer necessarily capped at a fixed low ceiling if the violation is deemed serious (e.g., unauthorized cross-border transfer of sensitive personal data, large-scale unauthorized disclosure, or repeated non-compliance after a warning).

Criminal liability: Vietnam's Penal Code (Article 288 on illegal provision or use of information on computer networks, and related provisions on trading in citizens' personal information) already criminalizes buying, selling, or illegally disclosing personal data, particularly financial and citizen ID data. Penalties can include fines up to several hundred million VND and imprisonment terms ranging from a few months up to 7 years for aggravated cases (e.g., data on 500+ people, or data used for fraud). Corporate officers and data controllers can face personal criminal exposure, not just the entity.

Administrative sanctions also commonly include: mandatory remediation orders, suspension of data processing activities, revocation of licenses for certain regulated sectors (telecoms, fintech, e-commerce platforms), public disclosure/naming of the violator, and confiscation of profits derived from illegal processing.

Sector-specific overlays: Banks and fintechs face additional exposure under State Bank of Vietnam regulations; telecom and OTT providers face MIC-specific sanctions; and any company operating in Vietnam without a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) dossier or Cross-Border Transfer Impact Assessment (TIA) on file, when one is legally required, faces an independent violation exposed regardless of whether an actual breach occurred — Vietnam's regime, unlike GDPR, penalizes procedural non-compliance (missing paperwork, missing consent records) even absent a breach or complaint.

Practical exposure for most mid-size foreign-invested companies: the realistic near-term risk isn't the theoretical 5%-of-revenue maximum but the base-tier administrative fines (typically 200–500 million VND per violation type, and violations are counted per data subject category or per incident, so they compound quickly), combined with mandatory corrective action orders that can halt data processing operations until remediated — often more costly in business disruption than the fine itself.

Recommendation: any company processing Vietnamese personal data should treat Decree 13/PDPL compliance as a documentation-and-process exercise first (DPIA dossier, consent records, data processing agreements, breach notification procedures within the 72-hour-equivalent window used in Vietnam), because enforcement to date has focused heavily on entities lacking basic compliance artifacts rather than on subtle interpretive violations. I'd recommend confirming the exact current fine caps and the PDPL's final effective provisions with a Vietnam-qualified lawyer, since implementing regulations and fine schedules were still being finalized through 2025–2026 and specific figures may have been adjusted in the final text.
