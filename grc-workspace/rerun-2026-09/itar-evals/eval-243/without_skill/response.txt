Short answer: I can't confirm the current status of USML Category XI(b) as of today without pulling the live text of 22 CFR 121.1 (the USML) and any recent Federal Register amendments, so treat what follows as background on how this category has historically worked and what to check, rather than a definitive "yes it's still controlled" or "no it's been removed" answer.

USML Category XI covers "Military Electronics," and subparagraph (b) has historically captured electronic systems not elsewhere enumerated that are specially designed for intelligence, early warning, signal/electronic warfare, or command-and-control functions—language broad enough that it has, at various points, been read to sweep in intelligence-analytics software, particularly software specially designed for intelligence analysis, target development, or similar defense-intelligence functions.

Here's why you might have heard the control was "temporary." Category XI(b) uses the "specially designed" catch-all construct, and DDTC/State has a long history of using temporary or interim rules, Federal Register notices with comment periods, and periodic export control reform initiatives to narrow or clarify catch-all categories that were originally drafted very broadly. During the Export Control Reform (ECR) initiative (the multi-year effort roughly 2013-2020 that moved many "less sensitive" military items from the USML to the Commerce Control List's 600-series), Category XI was one of the categories revised, and State has periodically issued proposed rules seeking public comment specifically on how "intelligence analytics" or "intelligence and information analysis" software should be scoped—because the term is inherently broad and risks capturing generic data-analytics or AI/ML software that has no defense-specific design intent.

Given that pattern, "the control was only temporary" plausibly refers to one of a few possible situations: (a) an interim final rule that was later finalized with revised, narrower language; (b) a proposed rule still in a comment/finalization process, meaning the "temporary" language remains in effect until a final rule is published; or (c) a sunset/expiration provision attached to a specific temporary designation that has since lapsed, potentially shifting jurisdiction for certain analytics software to the EAR (specifically the Commerce Control List, possibly ECCN 0Y521 series or another 600-series/XI-related ECCN) rather than the USML.

What this means practically for you: you cannot rely on general recollection or a vendor's past classification determination. The compliance-correct path is:

1. Pull the current, official text of USML Category XI(b) directly from 22 CFR 121.1 (via the eCFR) and cross-check against DDTC's public guidance and any recent Federal Register notices amending Category XI.
2. If your software's classification is genuinely ambiguous—which "specially designed for intelligence analysis" language often is—submit a Commodity Jurisdiction (CJ) request to DDTC to get an authoritative determination rather than relying on informal industry consensus.
3. Document your classification rationale in writing regardless of outcome, since "we believed it was decontrolled" is a weak defense in an enforcement context if the rationale wasn't documented and periodically revisited.
4. Watch for parallel EAR classification: if the software is genuinely dual-use or has been moved off the USML, it likely lands on the CCL under an ECCN in the 0Y521 series or a similar military-intelligence-adjacent category, which still carries meaningful licensing requirements, just under the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) rather than DDTC.
5. Reassess any past export or disclosure decisions made under the assumption of USML control (or non-control)—a classification change can retroactively affect whether past transfers were properly licensed, which matters for both compliance risk and any potential voluntary disclosure analysis.

Given the reputational and licensing stakes of getting intelligence-related software classification wrong, I'd treat this as a "verify, don't assume" situation: get current regulatory text, and if there's real ambiguity, use the CJ process or qualified export-control counsel rather than resolving it from memory or hearsay about what changed.
