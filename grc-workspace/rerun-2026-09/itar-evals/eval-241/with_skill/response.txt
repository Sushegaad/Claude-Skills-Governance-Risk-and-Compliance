Short answer: your DIRCM-modified 737s become somewhat easier to move as aircraft, but the DIRCM system itself and its technical data do not leave ITAR. Here is the analysis.

**Article description.** The article in question has two distinct pieces for jurisdiction purposes: (1) the Boeing 737 civil airframe as modified, and (2) the installed defensive aids suite (DIRCM turret, missile-warning sensors (IR/UV), associated processing and anti-tamper hardware/software).

**USML enumeration test (22 CFR § 121.1).** Historically, a civil FAA-certificated transport aircraft modified with self-protection/ASE equipment fell under USML Category VIII(a)(8), because the modification was treated as converting the whole aircraft into a defense article. The October 13, 2026 rule (91 FR 55457) revises VIII(a)(8) to carve out civil FAA transport/commuter category aircraft equipped with "secured ASE" — defined in new § 121.0 as DIRCM systems, or the IR/UV missile-warning sensors needed to cue DIRCM, provided the installation incorporates anti-tamper protection. If your 737s meet that definition (FAA transport-category airframe, DIRCM + missile-warning sensors, anti-tamper features per § 121.0), the aircraft itself is excluded from Category VIII(a)(8) as of the effective date.

**What does NOT move.** The rule is narrow. The ASE equipment — the DIRCM turret, sensor suite, associated processing, and anti-tamper components — remains separately controlled under Category XI(d) (military electronics / EW-adjacent equipment) regardless of what happens to the aircraft classification. All technical data and defense services tied to that ASE (installation drawings, source code, integration test data, maintenance/training support) stay ITAR-controlled under Category XI(d) and require a TAA (22 CFR § 124.1) for any foreign-person access, and a DSP-5/DSP-73 for any hardware export, exactly as before.

**Practical effect for VIP/head-of-state programs.** For the aircraft: if the modified 737 itself now falls outside VIII(a)(8), you may find the airframe transaction can proceed as a civil aircraft matter (potentially EAR/FAA export process for the airframe) rather than a full USML DSP-5 for the whole aircraft. But you cannot treat this as a "the whole program is now EAR" conclusion — that is the trap this rule is designed to catch. Two new exemptions matter operationally: § 120.54(a)(8) exempts temporary imports of secured ASE meeting the definition, and § 120.54(a)(9) exempts country-to-country movement of previously licensed, installed secured ASE — useful if you are repositioning already-licensed aircraft between allied VIP fleets without a new export authorization each time.

**Action items:**
1. Confirm your installation meets the full "secured ASE" definition in § 121.0, including anti-tamper — do not assume; document the engineering basis.
2. Keep the DIRCM/sensor hardware and all technical data under Category XI(d) controls: TAA in place for any foreign engineering/maintenance support, TCP coverage for any foreign-national engineers who touch ASE data.
3. Re-verify your product classification documentation (per your classification process) to reflect the split treatment — airframe vs. ASE — rather than a single "Category VIII" tag.
4. If ambiguity remains on whether your specific installation qualifies as "secured," consider a Commodity Jurisdiction request (22 CFR § 120.4) before relying on the exclusion, since comments on the rule were due September 28, 2026 and DDTC practice may still be settling.
5. Update your TAA/MLA and license tracking system to reflect the new exemptions at § 120.54(a)(8)–(9) so temporary imports and previously-licensed intra-fleet transfers aren't over-licensed going forward.

Bottom line answer rule: for aircraft-survivability/DIRCM/Cat VIII or XI jurisdiction questions after October 13, 2026, apply the revised VIII(a)(8) carve-out for the qualifying civil airframe, but always flag that the ASE equipment and its technical data remain ITAR-controlled under XI(d). Do not let the airframe carve-out be read as de-controlling the DIRCM system itself.

This is general compliance guidance, not legal advice — confirm your specific installation's eligibility for the carve-out with DDTC or qualified export-control counsel before changing your licensing posture.
