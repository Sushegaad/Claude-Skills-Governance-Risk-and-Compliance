The DDTC-BAE Systems, Inc. consent agreement (announced August 2026, $36M, with $18M suspended contingent on remedial spend) is one of the more instructive recent enforcement actions because it is a voluntary-disclosure case rather than a whistleblower or referral case — meaning DDTC's own reasoning about penalty mitigation is visible in the outcome. Key facts: 104 AECA/ITAR violations spanning May 2019 to March 2025, including 38 unauthorized technical-data exports to China (a § 126.1 issue — China is a proscribed destination, which normally drives aggravating-factor treatment), plus unauthorized exports and defense services across six additional countries. Terms: $36M total, $18M suspended for remedial compliance investment, a 3-year agreement term, a Special Compliance Officer, an external audit requirement, and — notably — no debarment.

**What drove the penalty (root causes DDTC cited):**
1. Weak written procedures — the violations reflect an absence of documented, repeatable export-control processes rather than a single rogue actor.
2. Inexperienced staff — personnel handling export decisions lacked adequate training or seniority to catch classification and authorization gaps.
3. Poor agreement management — TAA/MLA scope, parties, and authorized activities were not being tracked against actual conduct, so activity drifted outside licensed scope over time.
4. No authorization-tracking system — the company could not reliably answer "is this specific transfer covered by a current, valid license or agreement" at the point of transfer.

**What kept it from being worse — debarment avoided:** Two things: (1) voluntary self-disclosure (22 CFR § 127.12) and (2) cooperation with DDTC's investigation. This is the single clearest lesson: even 104 violations, including transfers to a proscribed destination, did not trigger debarment because the company found and reported the problem itself and cooperated fully. That is consistent with the mitigating-factor framework in Part 127 — VSD and cooperation are treated as heavily weighted mitigants, while national-security harm and pattern/volume of violations are aggravating factors that increase the dollar penalty but are not automatically debarment-triggering when mitigation is strong.

**Program design lessons to extract:**
- **Centralize authorization tracking.** Build (or buy) a system that maps every technical data disclosure, every hardware export, and every foreign-person interaction to a specific, currently valid license/agreement and its exact conditions (parties, scope, country, expiry). "We had a TAA" is not sufficient if the transfer fell outside that TAA's authorized scope or party list — this is exactly the "poor agreement management" finding.
- **Invest in staff experience/training commensurate with risk**, not just checkbox annual training. The BAE case implies personnel-level gaps, not just policy gaps — role-specific training for engineers and licensing staff (per the Training pillar of a compliance program) needs to be substantive, not pro forma.
- **Formalize written procedures** covering classification, screening, licensing, and record-keeping — DDTC treats the absence of documented process as a root cause, independent of whether any single violation was willful.
- **Treat § 126.1 proscribed-destination exposure as a standalone red flag** requiring its own control point (e.g., destination screening before any technical data leaves engineering, not just at shipment).
- **Build your VSD muscle now.** The consent agreement shows disclosure plus cooperation can be worth the difference between a large-but-survivable settlement and debarment. A documented internal escalation path so violations surface quickly (rather than being discovered years later) is what makes timely VSD possible in the first place.
- **Expect ongoing oversight as a term, not a one-time fine.** The Special Compliance Officer and external audit requirement show DDTC increasingly uses structural remedies (monitorship-style terms) even in negotiated settlements — build for sustained compliance investment, not a one-time check-writing event.

Use this case in your own gap assessment: score your program against the four root causes DDTC cited (procedures, staff, agreement management, authorization tracking) rather than only against the generic ITAR compliance checklist, since those are the specific failure modes this enforcement action confirms actually get penalized in practice. This is general compliance information, not legal advice — consult export-control counsel for your specific facts.
