Discovering two years of unauthorized ITAR exports is serious but very manageable if you handle the disclosure correctly—DDTC's Voluntary Self-Disclosure (VSD) process exists precisely for this situation and is generally treated as a strong mitigating factor in any resulting enforcement action, provided you follow the process properly. Here is the practical walk-through.

1. Stop the bleeding immediately, but don't panic-freeze the business. As soon as unauthorized exports are identified, convene your empowered official, legal counsel (ideally outside export control counsel with ITAR enforcement experience), and relevant business leaders to assess whether the activity is ongoing. If it is, halt any further unauthorized transfers, shipments, technical data disclosures, or defense services immediately. Do not, however, take drastic uncoordinated actions (e.g., abruptly canceling contracts or terminating employees) before understanding the full scope—that can complicate both the investigation and business/legal remediation.

2. Preserve documents and data. Issue a litigation/compliance hold across email, shipping records, engineering/technical data systems, contracts, and any communications related to the affected programs. Because this covers two years, you'll likely need IT and records management involved early to prevent normal retention-cycle deletion from destroying evidence you'll need for your own investigation and for DDTC.

3. Conduct an internal investigation to scope the violation before disclosing. DDTC expects disclosures to be reasonably complete, so rushing to file before you understand the facts often results in supplemental disclosures later (which is acceptable but less ideal than a thorough initial filing). Scope typically includes: what specific ITAR-controlled hardware, technical data, or defense services were involved; USML classification of each item; countries and end users involved; number and dates of transactions; root cause (e.g., misclassification, missing license, unauthorized foreign national access, agent/distributor issue, IT system gap); and whether any proscribed countries or denied/debarred parties were involved, which raises severity significantly.

4. Determine whether this is a "voluntary" disclosure under DDTC's standards. To get credit as a true VSD (22 CFR 127.12), the disclosure generally must be made before DDTC or another government agency independently discovers or is already investigating the violation, and it must be a full and complete initial notification followed by a thorough final report. If DDTC already has knowledge (e.g., through a tip, a customs hold, or another agency's referral), you may be in a different, less favorable posture, and counsel needs to assess this immediately—it affects strategy and expectations.

5. File the initial notification promptly. DDTC guidance calls for notifying them as soon as possible after the violation is discovered—historically within roughly 60 days is treated as a benchmark for "prompt," though the exact expectation should be confirmed with current DDTC guidance and your counsel, since practice on timing expectations can be refined over time. The initial notification is typically a short letter identifying that a violation occurred, a general description, and a commitment to investigate and file a full report.

6. Complete and file the full disclosure. This follow-up report (DDTC does not use a single mandated form; submissions are made per current DDTC electronic filing guidance) should include a detailed narrative of the violations, root cause analysis, list of affected transactions/parties/countries, classification determinations, and—critically—a remediation plan describing corrective actions already taken and planned (process fixes, training, system access controls, personnel actions if warranted, and internal control enhancements).

7. Build and execute a credible remediation plan in parallel, don't wait for DDTC's response. Common elements: corrected classification determinations with documentation; enhanced screening and licensing procedures; IT/access control fixes if technical data access was the root cause; retraining program managers, engineers, and shipping/logistics staff; and, depending on severity, resourcing enhancements to the compliance function itself. DDTC evaluates disclosures partly on whether the company demonstrates it has fixed the underlying control gap, not just the specific past transactions.

8. Expect a DDTC response that could range from a closing letter with no further action, to negotiated administrative penalties/consent agreement (especially given the two-year duration, which suggests either volume or a systemic control gap), to referral for further investigation in more serious cases (e.g., proscribed-country involvement or willfulness indicators). Voluntary, timely, complete disclosures with credible remediation are consistently treated more favorably in DDTC's penalty determinations than violations discovered through external means.

9. Parallel considerations: assess potential overlap with Commerce/BIS (EAR) jurisdiction if any items may not actually be ITAR-controlled, evaluate SEC/customer disclosure obligations if this is a public company or has government contracts with mandatory disclosure clauses (e.g., FAR/DFARS), and consider whether individual conduct raises personal liability issues requiring separate counsel for specific employees.

Because voluntary disclosures are legally and strategically consequential—and the specific procedural requirements can be updated by DDTC from time to time—I'd strongly recommend engaging export control counsel with DDTC enforcement experience before filing anything, so the initial notification and final report are structured correctly from the outset.
