This is a deemed export question, and the trigger is nationality, not physical location or work authorization status. Here is how the rule works and what to do about it.

**The core rule (22 CFR § 120.50).** A "deemed export" occurs the moment ITAR-controlled technical data (22 CFR § 120.33 — blueprints, drawings, specifications, source code, test reports, or oral/visual disclosure of equivalent information) is released to a foreign national, even if that release happens entirely inside the United States, in your own building. The release is legally treated as an export to that person's country of citizenship (or countries, if dual national), regardless of where the disclosure physically occurs.

**Who counts as "foreign national" here.** This is the part teams most often get wrong: it is about citizenship, not visa status, work authorization, or how long someone has worked for you. A green card holder (lawful permanent resident) is a "US person" under 22 CFR § 120.62 and is exempt from deemed-export licensing. Protected persons under 8 USC § 1324b are similarly treated as US persons. But an H-1B visa holder, an L-1 intracompany transferee, an F-1 student on OPT, or any employee who is a citizen only of a foreign country — even if fully work-authorized and even if they've been on your team for a decade — is a foreign person for this purpose. So the trigger question for each engineer is: "what is your citizenship (not your work authorization), and is it US, or LPR, or a protected-person category?" If the answer is "foreign citizen, no LPR/protected status," any ITAR technical data disclosure to them is a deemed export unless authorized.

**When it becomes "a problem" specifically:**
1. The moment a foreign-national employee is given access to a defense article's technical data — CAD files, source code, test data, design specs, even verbal walkthroughs in a design review — without a prior authorization in place. It does not require the employee to take the data anywhere; internal disclosure alone is the export.
2. It becomes a compliance failure (not just a risk) if that disclosure happens before you've confirmed no license is required for their country of citizenship, or before you've obtained the necessary TAA or individual license.
3. Country matters: some countries have streamlined pathways (e.g., certain UK/Australia/Canada treaty arrangements), but none of these are blanket exemptions — you must verify eligibility every time, and disclosures involving § 126.1 proscribed/embargoed countries (Belarus, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia, Syria, Venezuela) face the strictest scrutiny and often no path at all absent a presidential waiver.

**What to do before it becomes a problem:**
1. Inventory every foreign national with any potential access to ITAR-controlled data or controlled physical areas — capture citizenship (all citizenships if dual), not just work authorization.
2. For each one, determine whether their country of citizenship requires a license/TAA for the specific technical data involved.
3. Where required, obtain the TAA (22 CFR § 124.1) or individual license before granting any access — not after the fact. A TAA is required even for oral disclosure.
4. Stand up a Technology Control Plan (TCP): physical segregation of ITAR work areas, IT access controls/permissions tied to citizenship screening results, visitor/escort procedures for foreign-national visitors, and annual ITAR training for anyone with potential access.
5. Build citizenship screening into onboarding and project-staffing workflows so the check happens before a foreign-national engineer is added to an ITAR-relevant project team or repository — not as a retrospective audit finding.
6. Re-screen when someone's role, project assignment, or country of citizenship status changes (e.g., new dual citizenship, project transfer).

Bottom line: it becomes a problem at first disclosure without authorization, not at some later "point of harm" — so the compliance work has to happen at the staffing/access-provisioning stage, before any drawing, source file, or design conversation reaches a foreign-national engineer. This is general compliance guidance, not legal advice; consult export-control counsel to confirm treatment for specific employees, dual nationals, or ambiguous cases.
