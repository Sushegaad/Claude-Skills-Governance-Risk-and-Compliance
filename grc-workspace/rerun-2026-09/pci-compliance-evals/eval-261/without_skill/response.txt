Short answer: no, that is not acceptable, and if your QSA is signing off on it you should push back before the ROC goes anywhere near an acquirer or the card brands.

SAQ A is a self-assessment vehicle built for a very narrow population of merchants: those who have fully outsourced all cardholder data functions to PCI DSS validated third parties and who retain no cardholder data on their own systems, in any form, at any time — no capture, no transmission, no storage, not even transiently in server memory or logs. It exists so that the smallest, simplest e-commerce merchants (typically Level 4, sometimes Level 3) don't have to answer the full 300+ requirement set. It is a self-assessment questionnaire, not a Report on Compliance methodology, and it was never designed to be a scoping shortcut for an organization that undergoes a full ROC.

A Level 1 merchant, by definition, processes more than 6 million transactions a year (or has otherwise been designated Level 1 by a brand or acquirer) and is contractually required to validate compliance via a full ROC performed by a QSA using the PCI DSS Report on Compliance template — not any SAQ. The SAQs (A, A-EP, B, B-IP, C, C-VT, D for merchants, D for service providers, P2PE) are validation tools for merchants who are permitted to self-assess. Once you are a Level 1 merchant obligated to produce a ROC, the "applicability" of requirements is governed by the actual scope of your cardholder data environment (CDE) as determined by the standard scoping process in DSS v4.0 — not by which SAQ type your business model superficially resembles.

There is a related, legitimate concept that may be what's actually going on here: even within a full ROC, a QSA can mark individual requirements or sub-requirements as "Not Applicable" (N/A) when there is a well-documented, verifiable reason — for example, requirements about wireless security when the environment has no wireless technology anywhere in or connected to the CDE, or requirements about a technology the environment doesn't use. PCI DSS v4.0 formalizes this with the "Not Tested" and targeted risk analysis mechanisms and requires an N/A justification to be documented per requirement in the ROC (Appendix C in the reporting template, or equivalent). That is a control-by-control, evidence-based determination — fundamentally different from importing SAQ A's eligibility criteria (fully outsourced, no CDE touch) as a blanket excuse to mark most of Requirements 1 through 12 as N/A across the board.

If your environment genuinely has no cardholder data touching your systems — e.g., 100% iframe/redirect to a PCI DSS validated processor with no capability to alter that redirect, and no other CDE presence — that would need to be demonstrated network segment by network segment, system by system, and even then, many requirements (e.g., Requirement 12 governance items, Requirement 6.4.3 and 11.6.1 script/skimming controls for payment pages that were added specifically because "fully outsourced" merchants were being skimmed) still apply to Level 1 ROC merchants under DSS v4.0. Those two requirements in particular were added because the SAQ-A population itself was found to be a major skimming target, so even genuinely eligible merchants can't wave those away.

Practical implications and recommendations:
1. Ask the QSA to produce, in writing, the specific scoping analysis and evidence supporting each N/A designation, tied to actual architecture (network diagrams, data flow diagrams, segmentation testing results) — not to SAQ eligibility criteria.
2. Confirm with your acquirer what reporting they actually require for your validation level; they, not the QSA, are the ultimate recipient with authority over acceptable ROC scope.
3. Push for a full scoping exercise per PCI DSS v4.0 Requirement 12.5.2 (annual scope confirmation) — this is the only legitimate mechanism for excluding systems from the CDE.
4. If the QSA continues to apply SAQ eligibility logic to a ROC, consider escalating to the QSA company's quality assurance function or reporting the practice to the PCI SSC, since QSAs are bound by the QSA Qualification Requirements and can lose their qualification for methodologically unsound assessments.
5. Recognize the compliance risk: an acquirer or card brand discovering a ROC built on improper scoping can trigger revalidation, fines, or increased scrutiny — all worse outcomes than doing the scoping correctly now.

In short, SAQ A eligibility and ROC "Not Applicable" determinations are different instruments serving different populations and different levels of assurance; conflating them is a compliance and audit-quality problem, not a shortcut.
