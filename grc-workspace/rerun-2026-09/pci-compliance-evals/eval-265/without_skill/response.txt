These are two distinct mechanisms in PCI DSS for handling a requirement you can't meet exactly as written, and PCI DSS v4.0 changed the landscape significantly by adding the customized approach alongside the traditional compensating controls process. They serve different situations and have different documentation burdens.

Compensating Controls (the traditional route, still available in v4.0 as "defined approach with compensating controls"):
- Used when you cannot meet a requirement's defined approach exactly due to documented, legitimate technical or business constraints (e.g., a legacy system that can't support a specific control, or an application that would break if a specific configuration were applied).
- You must implement an alternative control that sufficiently mitigates the risk the original requirement was designed to address, meeting the intent and rigor of the original requirement.
- Requires completing a Compensating Controls Worksheet (found in the PCI DSS ROC template / SAQ instructions) for each instance, which must document: the constraint, the objective of the original requirement, the identified risk, the specific compensating control implemented, and how that control was validated as effective.
- Compensating controls must be re-evaluated and re-documented at every assessment cycle — they aren't a one-time fix, they need to be revisited to confirm the constraint still exists and the control is still effective.
- Compensating controls are generally viewed as a narrower, more constrained mechanism — meant for edge cases and legacy exceptions, not as a general design philosophy. QSAs scrutinize these heavily because historically they were sometimes used as a way to paper over gaps rather than genuinely equivalent security.

Customized Approach (new in PCI DSS v4.0, a first-class alternative to the "defined approach"):
- Every requirement in v4.0 now has two ways to satisfy it: the "defined approach" (the specific control described in the requirement, e.g., "passwords must be at least 12 characters") or the "customized approach" (design and implement your own control that meets the stated "Customized Approach Objective" published for that requirement, using whatever method you choose, provided it meets that objective).
- This is not a workaround for constraints — it is a deliberate, forward-looking design choice for organizations with mature security programs who want to use innovative or non-standard controls (e.g., risk-based or adaptive authentication instead of a fixed password complexity rule) as long as they can prove the underlying security objective is met.
- Requires a formal, documented risk analysis for each customized control, following the methodology PCI SSC published in v4.0 (documented in Appendix D / E of the ROC template), including: the control design, how it meets the stated objective, applicable risk factors, and testing procedures the QSA will use to validate it (since generic testing procedures obviously don't apply — the assessor has to develop testing procedures specific to your custom control).
- Only certain requirements are eligible for customized approach (some are marked as not eligible in the standard); check the "Applicability Notes" in the DSS itself.
- The customized approach generally requires significantly more assessment rigor and QSA judgment than the defined approach, and is intended for organizations with strong security engineering and risk management maturity — not a quick fix.

Key distinction to keep in mind:
- Compensating controls answer: "I can't do the defined thing because of a specific documented constraint, so here's an equivalent alternative that addresses the same risk." It's retrospective/exception-driven and revisited each cycle.
- Customized approach answers: "I choose not to do the defined thing at all, because my own control design meets the same underlying security objective, potentially better or differently, and I can prove it with a rigorous, methodology-driven risk analysis." It's proactive/design-driven, part of your standing control architecture, and requires a heavier evidentiary and testing burden up front (custom testing procedures the assessor must develop) but doesn't require you to have a "constraint" as a prerequisite.

Practical guidance for your situation:
1. First ask why you can't meet the requirement as written — is it a genuine technical/business constraint on a legacy system (compensating control territory), or is it that you have a genuinely better/different way of achieving the same security outcome (customized approach territory)?
2. If it's a temporary or system-specific limitation, go the compensating controls route — faster to document, well-understood by QSAs, but expect to redo the worksheet every assessment cycle and expect QSAs to push back if the constraint looks avoidable (e.g., "we haven't gotten around to patching this" is not a valid constraint).
3. If it's a mature, intentional alternative control design you want to standardize, engage your QSA early about customized approach — it requires collaboration on testing procedures before the assessment even starts, and your QSA needs specific customized-approach training/experience to assess it properly.
4. Either way, document everything: the objective of the original requirement, your risk analysis, the alternative/custom control, and validation evidence. Under-documentation is the most common reason either approach gets rejected during an assessment.
5. Don't assume every unmet requirement needs one of these two paths — first check whether the requirement genuinely applies to your environment at all (a scoping/applicability question) before treating it as a compensating-control or customized-approach case.
