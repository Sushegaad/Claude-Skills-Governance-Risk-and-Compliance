Scope reduction is almost always the highest-leverage compliance investment you can make, because every system, network segment, and person that touches or can influence cardholder data flow is "in scope" for the full PCI DSS control set — and with card data flowing through half your network, you're currently applying (or should be applying) all 12 requirement families to roughly half your infrastructure, which is expensive to assess, expensive to maintain, and a large attack surface.

Here is a practical approach:

1. Map the actual data flow first. Before touching architecture, do a rigorous data discovery and flow-mapping exercise: where does card data enter (POS, e-commerce, call center, file transfers, batch files, backups), where is it transmitted, where is it stored (including accidental storage — logs, temp files, database exports, email attachments, help desk ticketing systems), and where is it disposed of. Most scope bloat comes from data that shouldn't be there in the first place — legacy integrations, debug logging, analytics pipelines that accidentally capture PAN, or spreadsheets used by finance/support teams. Cleaning up unnecessary storage and flow is often the single biggest scope reduction you can achieve, before any network work.

2. Segment the network with validated controls. "Segmentation" for PCI purposes means using firewalls, ACLs, VLANs with enforced access control (not just VLAN tagging), or dedicated physical separation to isolate the cardholder data environment (CDE) from the rest of the network such that systems outside the CDE cannot access it. Segmentation must be validated through penetration testing (PCI DSS Requirement 11.4.5/11.4.6) at least annually and after significant changes, confirming that out-of-scope systems genuinely cannot reach in-scope systems. Without validated segmentation, PCI DSS treats your entire flat network as in-scope — which sounds like your current state.

3. Reduce the number of ways cardholder data enters your environment. Common techniques:
 - Point-to-point encryption (P2PE): validated P2PE solutions encrypt card data at the point of interaction (e.g., a PIN pad) such that your systems never see cleartext PAN; this can shrink certain merchant environments down to the smallest applicable SAQ (e.g., SAQ P2PE).
 - Tokenization: replace PAN with a non-sensitive token immediately after authorization, so downstream systems (loyalty, reporting, CRM, analytics) that only need to reference a transaction never need real PAN.
 - Outsource acceptance/processing to validated third parties: for e-commerce, moving to a hosted payment page or redirect (SAQ A/A-EP territory) removes your web servers from PAN handling. For call centers, using a dual-tone telephone masking/pause-and-resume recording solution keeps PAN out of recorded calls and agent desktops.
 - Eliminate unnecessary retention: if you don't have a documented business, legal, or regulatory need to store PAN, don't store it. Many organizations retain full PAN for chargebacks or reporting when a truncated PAN or token would suffice.

4. Isolate supporting/connected systems properly. Systems that don't store, process, or transmit CDE data but are connected to the CDE (jump boxes, monitoring servers, patch management servers, AD domain controllers used for CDE authentication) are still in scope as "connected-to" or "security-impacting" systems. Decide deliberately: either bring them under full CDE-level controls, or architect distinct, minimally-privileged access paths (dedicated jump hosts, separate AD forests/OUs, restricted service accounts) so they qualify for the lighter "connected-to" scope category rather than pulling your whole enterprise AD into scope.

5. Use a phased target-state architecture. A common pattern: (a) inventory and data-flow diagram current state, (b) define target CDE boundary with justification for every system inside it, (c) build segmentation controls (firewalls/ACLs) to enforce that boundary, (d) migrate/redesign flows so card data no longer needs to traverse out-of-scope segments (e.g., move to tokenized values for anything leaving the CDE), (e) validate with an internal test then formal penetration test, (f) update your PCI DSS scope confirmation (Requirement 12.5.2, done at least annually) to reflect the new, smaller boundary.

6. Watch for scope creep vectors that undo your work: shared credentials between in-scope and out-of-scope systems, backup jobs that pull CDE data to a general-purpose backup server, SIEM/logging pipelines that ingest raw CDE logs onto a shared platform, and remote access tools (RDP, VPN, remote support software) that bridge segments.

7. Get your QSA or ISA involved early in defining the target boundary — scope determination is ultimately validated by them, and it's far cheaper to get their agreement on the target architecture before you build it than to discover after implementation that your segmentation doesn't satisfy their definition of "adequately isolated."

Bottom line: don't think of this as one project — it's a combination of data minimization (stop capturing/storing what you don't need), technology substitution (tokenization, P2PE, outsourcing to validated providers), and enforced network segmentation, validated by testing. Organizations that do all three together typically go from "most of the network in scope" to a compact, well-defined CDE containing only the specific systems that truly need to touch cardholder data.
