PCI DSS v4.0 was published in March 2022 with a set of new requirements that were explicitly marked as "best practice until 31 March 2025" — meaning assessors did not have to test them as mandatory until that future date arrived. That date has now passed (we're well past it as of late 2025/2026), so all of those previously future-dated requirements are fully in effect and must be assessed as mandatory in any ROC or SAQ. There is no more grace period; if you haven't implemented them, you're non-compliant against the current version of the standard, since v3.2.1 was also retired (it was retired March 31, 2024, a full year before the future-dated requirements became mandatory).

The requirements that moved from "future-dated best practice" to "mandatory now" span nearly every major requirement family. The ones that tend to bite hardest in practice are:

1. Requirement 8.3.6 / 8.6.3 — password and authentication strengthening. Minimum password length increased to 12 characters (up from 7), and there are new requirements around protecting against the use of compromised credentials and stricter controls on shared/generic accounts and interactive login for accounts used by applications and systems. Many organizations have legacy systems or embedded devices that can't easily support 12-character minimums, or have never inventoried where shared credentials are used.

2. Requirement 8.4.2 — multi-factor authentication (MFA) for all access into the CDE, not just remote/administrative access as under v3.2.1, but now for all access by personnel with access to the CDE, including from within the corporate network. This is one of the most operationally disruptive changes because it requires MFA rollout to internal, non-remote access paths that many companies never had MFA on.

3. Requirement 3.5.1.1 / 3.6.1.1 — stronger cryptographic key management practices, including requirements around key custodianship and use of a documented key management strategy, particularly problematic for organizations with ad hoc or undocumented key rotation practices.

4. Requirement 6.4.3 — for merchants with payment pages (including SAQ A merchants), a formal inventory of all scripts loaded on the payment page, with justification for each script's business necessity and a mechanism to confirm the integrity of each authorized script. This has hit e-commerce merchants especially hard because most have never inventoried third-party JavaScript (analytics, chat widgets, ad tech, tag managers) running on checkout pages, and Magecart-style skimming attacks specifically exploit unmonitored third-party scripts.

5. Requirement 11.6.1 — a change- and tamper-detection mechanism to alert personnel of unauthorized modifications to the HTTP headers and content of payment pages, evaluated at least weekly. This requires actual tooling (many organizations use a specialized script/DOM monitoring service) that most merchants didn't previously have.

6. Requirement 10.7.1 / 10.7.2 / 10.7.3 — detection and response to failures of critical security control systems (firewalls, IDS/IPS, FIM, anti-malware, physical access controls, logging mechanisms, segmentation controls, audit log review mechanisms, automated security testing tools) with defined response procedures. This requires formal monitoring of the security tools themselves, not just the environment they protect.

7. Requirement 12.3.1 to 12.3.4 (targeted risk analysis) — where the standard now allows organizations to define their own frequency for certain activities (like log reviews, POI device inspections) based on a documented targeted risk analysis, rather than a fixed PCI-mandated interval. This is flexible but requires new formal risk-analysis documentation many orgs haven't built yet.

8. Requirement 5.4.1 — anti-phishing mechanisms to detect and protect personnel against phishing attacks, going beyond traditional anti-malware.

9. Requirement 7.2.4 — periodic review of user accounts and access privileges (recertification), formalized and made explicit.

Why these bite hardest: MFA-everywhere (8.4.2) and password length (8.3.6) touch every system and every user, often colliding with legacy application constraints. The payment page script/tamper controls (6.4.3, 11.6.1) require net-new tooling and vendor management for e-commerce merchants, including smaller SAQ A merchants who assumed "we're fully outsourced" meant nothing new applied to them. Security-control-failure response (10.7.x) requires operational maturity — documented procedures and evidence of actual incident handling — not just technology deployment.

Practical next step: if you haven't already done a gap assessment specifically against these formerly future-dated requirements, that should be the immediate priority, since assessors are now required to test them as fully in-force controls, and any gaps will show up as failed/non-compliant items on your next ROC or SAQ rather than as "future-dated, not yet assessed."
