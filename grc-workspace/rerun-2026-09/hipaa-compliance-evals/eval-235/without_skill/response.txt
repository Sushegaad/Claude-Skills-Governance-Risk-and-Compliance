A cloud vendor hosting your EHR is a business associate under HIPAA — even if it never actually opens or views the PHI, the mere fact that it stores or transmits ePHI on your behalf triggers the requirement — so you need a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) before any PHI touches that environment, not as a formality after the fact. Here's what needs to be in it, drawing on the required elements at 45 CFR §164.314(a) and §164.504(e).

1. Permitted uses and disclosures. The BAA must specify exactly what the vendor is allowed to do with the PHI — generally limited to providing the hosting/EHR service itself, plus narrowly defined activities like the vendor's own management/administration or legal responsibilities, and data aggregation if applicable. It should explicitly prohibit any use or disclosure beyond what the underlying services agreement requires, and prohibit any use for the vendor's own marketing, product development, or resale purposes unless you've separately and explicitly agreed to that (and even then, additional authorization requirements may apply).

2. Safeguards commitment. The vendor must agree to implement appropriate administrative, physical, and technical safeguards to prevent unauthorized use or disclosure of the PHI, and — since this is ePHI hosted in the cloud — the BAA should explicitly incorporate compliance with the Security Rule's safeguards (access controls, audit controls, integrity controls, transmission security, encryption where appropriate), not just the general Privacy Rule safeguard language.

3. Reporting obligations. The BAA must require the vendor to report to you any use or disclosure not permitted by the agreement, and — critically — any security incident it becomes aware of, and any breach of unsecured PHI, within a specified timeframe. Since you as the covered entity have your own 60-day breach notification clock to patients/HHS, your BAA needs a tighter internal deadline for the vendor to notify you (many organizations negotiate 24-72 hours for the vendor to report a known or suspected breach) so you have runway to meet your own obligations.

4. Subcontractor flow-down. If the cloud vendor uses its own subcontractors that will have access to PHI (e.g., a subprocessor providing infrastructure, backup, or support services), the BAA must require the vendor to ensure those subcontractors agree to the same restrictions and conditions via their own BAA-equivalent agreements. This is especially relevant for cloud hosting, where infrastructure providers (underlying cloud platform), monitoring tools, backup vendors, and support/ticketing systems may all touch PHI indirectly.

5. Individual rights support. The agreement must require the vendor to make PHI available to you (or, per your instructions, directly to the individual) to support patient access requests, and to make amendments to PHI as directed, and to provide an accounting of disclosures as needed to support your obligations under the Privacy Rule.

6. Availability to HHS. The vendor must agree to make its internal practices, books, and records relating to PHI use/disclosure available to HHS for compliance determination purposes.

7. Return or destruction of PHI at termination. At the end of the relationship, the BAA must require the vendor to return or destroy all PHI it holds, if feasible; if return/destruction isn't feasible (a real issue with cloud backups and replicated storage), the agreement must extend the same protections to the retained PHI for as long as it's retained and limit further uses/disclosures to those purposes that made return/destruction infeasible.

8. Termination rights. You need the right to terminate the agreement if the vendor materially violates the BAA terms, and if termination isn't feasible, the right to report the violation to HHS.

Beyond the strict regulatory minimums, a few practical items worth negotiating specifically for a cloud EHR hosting arrangement: data residency/location commitments (where the data centers are physically located), encryption specifications (at rest and in transit, key management ownership), audit rights (your ability to request SOC 2 reports, penetration test summaries, or conduct your own security assessment), data portability/export commitments so you're not locked in if you switch vendors, defined uptime/availability SLAs since this is your EHR, cyber insurance requirements, and indemnification allocation for breach-related costs (notification, credit monitoring, regulatory fines attributable to the vendor's failure).

Before you sign, confirm this BAA is a standalone agreement or a properly incorporated addendum/exhibit to the master services agreement, that it doesn't conflict with liability caps or indemnification language elsewhere in the contract, and that your own IT/security team has actually validated the technical safeguard claims (don't just accept a BAA's promises — request the vendor's HITRUST certification, SOC 2 Type II report, or equivalent independent attestation).

This is general guidance, not legal advice — given how central an EHR hosting relationship is to your compliance posture, I'd have healthcare/HIPAA counsel review the final BAA and its interplay with the broader services contract before execution.
