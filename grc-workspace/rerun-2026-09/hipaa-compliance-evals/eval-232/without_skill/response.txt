Yes — OCR does enforce HIPAA against self-funded, employer-sponsored group health plans, and this is one of the more commonly misunderstood corners of HIPAA applicability. The plan itself is a "covered entity" under the statute (specifically, a "health plan" as defined at 45 CFR §160.103), regardless of whether it's insured or self-funded. The employer as a business (the entity that pays wages, runs payroll, sells widgets, etc.) is not a covered entity, but the group health plan it sponsors is a separate legal construct for HIPAA purposes and is squarely within OCR's jurisdiction.

A few points that matter for your specific situation:

1. Self-funded does not mean exempt. People sometimes assume HIPAA "enforcement" is aimed at hospitals, doctors, clearinghouses, and insurance carriers, and that self-funded plans fly under the radar because there's no insurance company standing between the employer and the risk. That's incorrect. OCR's enforcement database and resolution agreements include self-funded plan sponsors, and the statute draws no distinction based on funding mechanism.

2. There is a narrow exception for very small self-administered plans. If a self-funded plan has fewer than 50 participants and is administered solely by the employer that established it (no third-party administrator at all), it is excluded from the HIPAA definition of "health plan." That's a narrow carve-out — the moment you use a third-party administrator, ASO arrangement, PBM, or any outside claims administrator (which is true of the overwhelming majority of self-funded plans), the exception doesn't apply and the plan is a covered entity.

3. The plan and the plan sponsor (employer) are legally distinct, and the rules require you to maintain that separation. If your health plan wants to share PHI with the employer for plan administration purposes (e.g., HR handling appeals, benefits staff resolving enrollment questions), the plan document must contain specific HIPAA "plan sponsor" provisions restricting use/disclosure to plan administration functions, requiring a firewall between employees who touch PHI and those who make employment decisions, and prohibiting use of PHI for employment-related actions or other benefit plans. Many employers get tripped up here — HR staff who also handle hiring/firing/promotions inappropriately having access to health claims data is a classic exposure.

4. Enforcement realities: OCR's Security Rule audits and breach investigations of self-funded plans are less frequent per-capita than for hospitals simply because there are more provider entities and more patient-facing incidents that generate complaints, but self-funded plans absolutely get investigated, especially following (a) a breach report (which is mandatory for breaches of unsecured PHI affecting the plan's participants), (b) a participant complaint to OCR, or (c) findings during audits of the TPA/ASO that surface plan sponsor deficiencies.

5. Business associate exposure flows both ways. Your TPA, stop-loss carrier data-sharing arrangements, wellness vendors, and any vendor touching claims data need BAAs. If your TPA has a breach, your plan (as the covered entity) typically bears breach-notification obligations to affected plan participants, even though the TPA caused it — so your BAA needs to clearly define notification timelines, cost allocation, and cooperation duties.

Practically, what I'd suggest for a self-funded employer plan: confirm you have HIPAA privacy and security policies specific to the plan (not just a generic HR privacy policy), confirm your plan document has the required plan-sponsor certification language if PHI flows to HR, confirm BAAs exist with your TPA/ASO, PBM, stop-loss carrier (to the extent PHI is shared), and any wellness or EAP vendor, designate a privacy official for the plan, and make sure whoever administers COBRA, appeals, or claims disputes on the employer side has been trained and is walled off from employment-decision functions. If you haven't done a HIPAA risk analysis specific to the plan's data flows (versus just relying on the TPA's own compliance), that's usually the biggest gap I see in self-funded employer plans, and it's exactly the kind of gap that surfaces during an OCR investigation after a breach.

This is general compliance information, not legal advice — for a definitive read on your plan's specific structure (fully self-administered vs. TPA-administered, size, funding arrangement), I'd loop in ERISA/HIPAA counsel.
