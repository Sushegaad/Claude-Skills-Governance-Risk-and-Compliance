This is a real compliance failure, not a gray area, and eight months is far enough past the deadline that you should treat it as an active exposure needing immediate remediation rather than something to quietly finish whenever convenient.

Here's the baseline rule: under the HIPAA Privacy Rule's right of access provision (45 CFR §164.524), a covered entity must act on a request for records within 30 calendar days of receipt. There is a single allowable 30-day extension, but only if you notify the individual in writing, within the original 30-day period, of the reason for the delay and the date by which you will provide the records. That means the absolute outer bound, even using the extension properly, is 60 days. If no extension notice was ever sent, the outer bound was 30 days. Either way, at eight months (roughly 240 days) you are somewhere between 180 and 210 days past even the most generous legitimate deadline, with apparently no extension notice on record based on how you've described it.

Why this matters more than it might seem: OCR has run a standalone "Right of Access Initiative" since 2019 specifically targeting slow or non-responsive record requests, and it has resulted in dozens of settlements, many against small and mid-sized providers, with penalties ranging from roughly $15,000 up to $200,000+ per case, plus corrective action plans with multi-year OCR monitoring. Delay length is one of the factors OCR weighs, and several settled cases involved delays in the several-months range similar to yours. This is one of the few HIPAA areas where OCR has been aggressively and publicly proactive rather than purely complaint-driven, so "we're a small practice, nobody will notice" is not a safe assumption.

Your exposure has several layers:

1. Regulatory: if the patient or someone on their behalf files a complaint with OCR (or the relevant state attorney general, many of which now also enforce patient access independently), you're a strong candidate for an investigation, and the fact pattern — flat non-response for eight months, no apparent extension notice — is exactly the profile OCR has pursued before. Corrective action plans following these settlements often include mandatory policy revisions, staff retraining, and 1-3 years of self-reporting to OCR, which is its own operational burden beyond the fine.

2. Reputational/relationship: an aggrieved patient waiting eight months for their own records is likely to escalate — complaint to OCR, complaint to your state licensing board, negative reviews, or in some states a private right of action or small-claims style remedy tied to state medical-records statutes (which often have their own separate, sometimes shorter, deadlines and penalty structures layered on top of HIPAA).

3. Operational root cause: an eight-month gap usually signals something is broken procedurally — a request that fell through the cracks, no tracking system for access requests, unclear ownership of who processes them, or a bottleneck (e.g., waiting on a EHR vendor export, or requiring in-person pickup that never got scheduled). If it happened once, it's worth checking whether other requests are similarly stuck.

What I'd do right now, in order: (1) produce the records to the patient immediately — do not wait for a complaint to force it, since remediation speed is one of the factors OCR and courts consider in assessing severity; (2) draft an apology/transparency communication to the patient - not an admission of specific legal violations without counsel's input, but acknowledgment of the delay and confirmation the records are now provided; (3) preserve your internal records of when the request was made, why it stalled, and what changed to finally produce it, since you'll want this if OCR or the patient does follow up; (4) audit whether any other outstanding access requests exist in your system right now with the same problem; (5) fix the process — implement a tracked intake log for access requests with calendar reminders at day 20 (to trigger extension review) and day 28 (to force action or a documented extension letter); and (6) loop in HIPAA/healthcare counsel now, proactively, rather than after a complaint arrives, especially if this patient seems litigious or has already threatened to escalate — counsel can help you decide whether any voluntary disclosure or self-report to OCR is advisable given your specific facts.

I'm not a lawyer, and given real penalty exposure here, this is a case where you want counsel's eyes on the specific facts (what the request said, whether any extension notice went out, your state's own medical records statute) before you finalize your response strategy.
