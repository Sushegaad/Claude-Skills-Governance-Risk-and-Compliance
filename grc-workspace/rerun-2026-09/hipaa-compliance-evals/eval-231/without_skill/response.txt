Short answer: nothing has taken effect yet that would require you to implement MFA and encryption today under a new binding rule, but you should still be moving toward both because a proposal exists and, separately, the current rule already pushes you strongly in that direction.

Here's the more precise picture. HHS's Office for Civil Rights (OCR) published a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) in early January 2025 to substantially overhaul the HIPAA Security Rule. That proposal would, among other things, eliminate the long-standing distinction between "required" and "addressable" implementation specifications and make many safeguards that are currently addressable — including things functionally similar to multi-factor authentication and encryption of ePHI at rest and in transit — mandatory rather than optional-with-justification. It would also add explicit requirements for network segmentation, vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, and annual compliance audits.

However, a proposed rule is not law. It has to go through notice-and-comment rulemaking, and then HHS has to issue a final rule with its own effective and compliance dates (historically HIPAA final rules have given covered entities and business associates something like 180 days to a year to come into compliance after publication). As of now, no final rule has been published, so there is no new enforceable MFA/encryption mandate in effect. You should not represent to auditors, business partners, or your board that "the new Security Rule" is currently binding — it isn't yet.

That said, three things matter practically:

1. The current Security Rule (45 CFR §164.312) already lists encryption and decryption as an "addressable" implementation specification, and access control requires "unique user identification." Addressable does not mean optional — it means you must implement it unless you document a formal risk-based rationale for why it's not reasonable and appropriate for your environment, and adopt an equivalent alternative safeguard if you skip it. In practice, OCR enforcement actions and resolution agreements over the last decade have repeatedly penalized entities that had unencrypted PHI on laptops, servers, or backups with no compensating controls and no documented rationale. So even without the new rule, "we didn't encrypt because it was addressable" is a weak defense that has burned organizations in real settlements.

2. Multi-factor authentication isn't separately named in the current rule, but it's widely treated as an industry-standard control for remote access, privileged accounts, and email/cloud systems, and its absence is frequently cited as a contributing factor in breach investigations (e.g., business email compromise, ransomware entry via RDP). Cyber insurers, and many federal and state guidance documents, already effectively require it as a baseline control.

3. Because rulemaking of this kind rarely gets weaker between proposal and final rule, treating MFA and encryption as coming requirements — and building them now — is a low-regret move. Retrofitting encryption across systems and rolling out MFA org-wide takes real project time, and getting ahead of it avoids a compressed compliance sprint later.

What I'd recommend concretely: (1) don't tell stakeholders you are legally required to comply with the NPRM today — you're not; (2) update your risk analysis to explicitly document your current position on encryption and MFA as addressable/best-practice items, with rationale if anything is not yet implemented; (3) start or continue a project to implement encryption at rest and in transit for ePHI and MFA for all remote, administrative, and email access, since these are very likely to become mandatory and are good security hygiene regardless; (4) monitor the Federal Register and HHS OCR's site for the final rule's publication, since that will start the compliance-date clock; and (5) if you want to be safe from a documentation standpoint, have counsel or your compliance lead memorialize today's date and current control state so you can show a clear "before" baseline once the final rule lands.

I'm not a licensed attorney, and if this proposed rule has significant implications for your organization's budget or timeline, it's worth having outside HIPAA counsel confirm the current status before you communicate anything definitive internally.
