# FedRAMP Vulnerability Remediation Timeframes and Writing a Proper POA&M Entry

## FedRAMP Remediation Timeframes

FedRAMP requires vulnerabilities identified through scanning (or other means, such as penetration testing) to be remediated within specific timeframes based on severity, counted from the date the vulnerability is **detected** (not discovered by the vendor separately, but per the official scan/report date):

| Severity | Remediation Timeframe |
|---|---|
| **Critical** | Within **15 days** |
| **High** | Within **30 days** |
| **Moderate/Medium** | Within **90 days** |
| **Low** | Typically tracked but with more flexible timelines; often expected to be remediated within a reasonable operational timeframe, though FedRAMP has historically focused mandatory SLA enforcement on Critical/High/Moderate. Confirm current guidance, as Low-severity handling expectations can vary by agency/ConMon requirements. |

**Important notes:**
- These clocks generally start from the date of **detection** (i.e., the scan date that identified the vulnerability), not the date the report is delivered or reviewed internally.
- If a finding cannot be remediated within the required timeframe, it must be documented as an open POA&M item with a justified, risk-based extended timeline and appropriate interim mitigations — simply missing the deadline without documentation is a compliance failure in itself.
- Repeated or excessive **overdue** POA&M items (especially Critical/High findings past their remediation deadline) are a common cause of ConMon escalations, authorization risk, and can jeopardize an agency's confidence in the CSP's security program.
- Deviation requests (sometimes called risk adjustments or false positive determinations) may be submitted when a finding is not exploitable in context, but these require justification and are subject to review/approval — they are not a default extension mechanism.

## How to Write a Proper POA&M Entry

A POA&M entry should be complete enough that an outside reviewer (agency ISSO, 3PAO, or FedRAMP PMO) can understand the risk, the plan, and the timeline without needing to ask follow-up questions. Standard FedRAMP POA&M template fields typically include:

1. **POA&M ID** — Unique identifier for tracking.
2. **Controls** — The NIST SP 800-53 control(s) affected (e.g., RA-5 Vulnerability Scanning, SI-2 Flaw Remediation).
3. **Weakness/Vulnerability Description** — Clear, specific description of the finding (e.g., CVE identifier, affected asset, the specific vulnerability, and how it was identified — scan tool name and scan date).
4. **Source/Identifying Party** — How the weakness was found (e.g., automated vulnerability scan, penetration test, 3PAO assessment, internal audit).
5. **Asset Identifier** — The specific system component, host, IP address, or hostname affected.
6. **Risk Rating/Severity** — Critical, High, Moderate, or Low, typically based on CVSS score mapped to FedRAMP severity categories.
7. **Adjusted Risk Rating (if applicable)** — If the CSP has performed a risk adjustment (e.g., compensating controls reduce actual exploitability), this should be documented with justification, distinct from the raw scanner-assigned severity.
8. **Mitigating Factors / Compensating Controls** — Any factors that reduce risk while the finding remains open (e.g., the vulnerable service is not internet-facing, WAF rules block the attack vector, network segmentation limits blast radius).
9. **Remediation Plan** — Specific, actionable steps being taken to resolve the finding (e.g., "Apply vendor patch X.Y.Z to affected hosts" or "Upgrade PostgreSQL to version N to resolve CVE-XXXX-XXXXX").
10. **Resources Required** — What's needed to complete remediation (e.g., maintenance window, vendor patch availability, budget/staffing).
11. **Scheduled Completion Date** — The original planned remediation date, calculated per the required SLA (15/30/90 days from detection).
12. **Milestones** — Interim steps/dates showing progress (e.g., "Patch tested in staging — [date]," "Patch scheduled for production deployment — [date]").
13. **Actual Completion Date** — Filled in once remediated, along with evidence (e.g., rescan results showing the finding cleared).
14. **Status** — Open, In Progress, Completed, Risk Accepted/Operationally Required, or Deviation Request approved.
15. **Original Detection Date** — Critical for calculating whether the item is on-time or overdue against the 15/30/90-day SLA.
16. **Vendor Dependency (if applicable)** — Note if remediation depends on a third-party vendor patch, and reference any vendor communication/timeline.

### Example POA&M Entry (Illustrative)

> **POA&M ID:** POA-2026-0147
> **Control(s):** SI-2, RA-5
> **Weakness Description:** Critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-XXXXX, CVSS 9.8) identified on host `app-prod-03` (10.x.x.x) via authenticated vulnerability scan (Tenable Nessus, scan date: 2026-08-01) — outdated OpenSSL library vulnerable to remote code execution.
> **Source:** Monthly authenticated vulnerability scan
> **Asset:** app-prod-03 / Application Tier (within system boundary)
> **Original Risk Rating:** Critical
> **Adjusted Risk Rating:** N/A (no compensating controls sufficient to downgrade)
> **Mitigating Factors:** Host is behind WAF; outbound rules restrict lateral movement; not yet observed exploited in the wild per vendor advisory.
> **Remediation Plan:** Upgrade OpenSSL to patched version 3.x.x on all affected hosts via configuration management pipeline; validate via rescan.
> **Resources Required:** Standard maintenance window; no additional budget.
> **Scheduled Completion Date:** 2026-08-16 (15 calendar days from 2026-08-01 detection date, per FedRAMP Critical SLA)
> **Milestones:** Patch validated in staging — 2026-08-08; Production deployment — 2026-08-14; Rescan confirmation — 2026-08-16.
> **Status:** In Progress
> **Detection Date:** 2026-08-01

### Best Practices for Managing This Specific POA&M Item (Critical Findings Not Remediated in Time)

Since your item specifically flags **Critical vulnerabilities not remediated within required timeframes**, treat this as a process failure, not just a technical one:

1. **Root-cause the SLA miss** — was it a patch-availability issue, a testing/change-management bottleneck, resource constraint, or a scanning/tracking gap where findings weren't triaged promptly? Document this in your remediation narrative, since agencies and 3PAOs will ask.
2. **Update the POA&M immediately** with a revised, realistic completion date and clear milestones — don't leave it silently overdue.
3. **Document compensating controls** in place while the item remains open, to demonstrate the residual risk is being actively managed.
4. **Review your vulnerability management process** (SI-2, RA-5 control implementation) — an ongoing pattern of missed Critical/High SLAs often triggers deeper agency scrutiny of your overall vulnerability management program, potentially including corrective action plans beyond the individual POA&M item.
5. **Communicate proactively** with your Authorizing Official/ISSO rather than waiting for them to flag the overdue item — this is generally viewed favorably compared to being caught by ConMon reporting.

## Bottom Line
FedRAMP requires Critical vulnerabilities remediated within **15 days**, High within **30 days**, and Moderate within **90 days** of detection. A proper POA&M entry documents the weakness, affected asset, risk rating, mitigating factors, a specific remediation plan with milestones, and both scheduled and actual completion dates — and for a Critical item that has already missed its SLA, you should also document root cause and compensating controls to demonstrate the risk is being actively managed while remediation is completed.
