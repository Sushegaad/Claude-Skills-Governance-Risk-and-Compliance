# CBUAE-licensed bank moving core banking to a cloud provider with Bahrain data centers

## Short answer

This is a **material/critical outsourcing arrangement** involving both **cloud outsourcing** and **cross-border data transfer**, and it will require **prior engagement with, and very likely prior written no-objection/approval from, the Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE)** before you proceed — plus a robust risk assessment addressing data residency, business continuity, and regulatory access considerations, since the data centers sit outside the UAE.

## Key regulatory layers

1. **CBUAE Outsourcing Regulation.** CBUAE maintains outsourcing rules for licensed financial institutions that classify outsourcing arrangements by materiality/criticality. Core banking systems and customer data processing are about as "material/critical" as an outsourcing arrangement gets — this triggers the highest tier of governance: board/senior management approval, a documented risk assessment, due diligence on the provider, contractual protections (audit rights, sub-outsourcing controls, exit strategy, business continuity commitments), and — critically — **prior notification to, and generally prior no-objection from, the CBUAE** before the arrangement goes live, given it involves core systems.
2. **Cloud-specific and IT/technology risk expectations.** CBUAE has specific expectations (through outsourcing rules and broader IT/cyber risk guidance) for cloud arrangements: data classification, encryption, key management, access controls, resilience/DR testing, concentration risk assessment (i.e., how much of your critical infrastructure depends on a single provider/region), and a credible exit strategy if the cloud arrangement needs to be unwound.
3. **Cross-border data location — Bahrain specifically.** Because the data centers are in Bahrain (outside the UAE), this raises two separate but related concerns:
   - **CBUAE data residency/access expectations:** Regulators generally want assurance that they retain effective supervisory access to data and systems even when hosted abroad, and historically CBUAE (like many central banks) has been cautious about core banking data and critical records leaving the UAE without safeguards — some expectations may require local backup/redundancy of critical data within the UAE, or specific contractual commitments guaranteeing CBUAE's access/audit rights notwithstanding offshore hosting.
   - **UAE federal PDPL cross-border transfer rules:** Customer personal data is also subject to the PDPL's cross-border transfer conditions — transfer to Bahrain would need to rely on an adequacy determination (if Bahrain is recognized), appropriate safeguards (contractual clauses/binding rules), a specific Data Office permit, or a narrower statutory exception. Don't assume GCC-to-GCC transfer is automatically frictionless; treat it as a transfer requiring an active compliance mechanism until confirmed otherwise.
4. **Bahrain-side considerations.** If the cloud/data center provider is itself licensed or regulated in Bahrain (e.g., interacting with the Central Bank of Bahrain's own outsourcing/cloud rules), you may also need to factor in Bahrain regulatory constraints on the provider's side — though the primary compliance burden sits with you as the CBUAE-licensed entity.

## What approvals/steps to expect

1. **Materiality assessment and board approval.** Document why this is a material/critical outsourcing arrangement, with a formal risk assessment (operational, concentration, cyber, legal/regulatory, exit).
2. **Due diligence on the provider.** Financial strength, security certifications, sub-outsourcing chain, jurisdictional risk (Bahrain's legal/regulatory environment, government access laws), and historical incident record.
3. **CBUAE engagement — notify and seek no-objection.** Given the "core banking system" scope, plan for a formal submission to CBUAE describing the arrangement, risk mitigations, data location, security controls, and exit plan, and expect this to require CBUAE sign-off before go-live rather than a simple post-hoc notification.
4. **Contractual protections.** Right to audit (including by CBUAE or its appointed auditors), data location and sub-processing transparency, breach notification obligations flowing through to you (and then to CBUAE), termination/exit assistance, and business continuity/DR commitments meeting CBUAE resilience expectations.
5. **Data residency mitigations.** Consider whether a local UAE backup/DR copy of critical data, or a hybrid architecture keeping the most sensitive data/processing in-UAE while using Bahrain for other workloads, reduces regulatory friction — this is often the practical compromise banks use rather than full offshore migration of core systems.
6. **PDPL cross-border transfer mechanism.** Independently document the legal basis for the customer data transfer to Bahrain (safeguards/contractual clauses at minimum), regardless of the CBUAE outsourcing approval track — these are two separate compliance obligations that both need to be satisfied.
7. **Ongoing monitoring.** Post-approval, expect continuous oversight obligations — periodic risk reassessment, incident reporting to CBUAE, and monitoring of the provider's control environment (e.g., SOC 2 reports, penetration test results).

## Bottom line

Treat this as a two-track approval problem: **(1)** CBUAE outsourcing approval/no-objection for a material/critical cloud outsourcing of core banking systems, with strong emphasis on resilience, concentration risk, and CBUAE's continued supervisory access; and **(2)** PDPL-compliant cross-border transfer mechanism for the customer personal data itself moving to Bahrain. Both should be resolved — ideally with a documented risk assessment and board approval — before the migration proceeds, not after.

*General guidance based on the CBUAE outsourcing regulatory framework and UAE federal PDPL cross-border transfer principles; given the scale (core banking migration, cross-border, regulated bank), confirm current CBUAE outsourcing circular requirements, thresholds for "material" outsourcing, and any Bahrain-specific considerations with UAE banking regulatory counsel before proceeding.*
