Recommendation: if you are a GPAI model provider that places a model on the EU market, sign the Code of Practice unless you have a documented, resourced alternative. For most providers, signing is the cheapest route to demonstrating compliance. It does not guarantee compliance or immunity.

Who has signed (as of November 2026)
- 22 full signatories per the Commission page updated 7 October 2026. They include Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI, and OpenAI.
- xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter. It must show compliance with the Transparency and Copyright obligations by alternative means.
- Verify the live list on the Commission page, because membership can change.

What the Code is
- Published 10 July 2025 and endorsed by the Commission and AI Board on 1 August 2025. It is voluntary.
- Three chapters: (1) Transparency, including the Model Documentation Form; (2) Copyright, including a written policy, respect for robots.txt and other opt-out protocols, and a complaints mechanism; (3) Safety and Security, which applies only to systemic-risk models.
- A Signatory Taskforce chaired by the AI Office supports implementation.

What signing gets you
1. A recognised way to demonstrate compliance with Arts. 53 and 55. The Commission treats adherence as a basis for assessing compliance. It is not a formal legal presumption in the way a cited harmonised standard is.
2. Regulatory predictability. AI Office enforcement powers over GPAI providers have applied since 2 August 2026, and Art. 91 information requests have started (first reported late August 2026, with another wave announced 14 October 2026). Signatories can point to a structured, agreed approach, and enforcement is expected to be more targeted at those who do not follow the Code.
3. Lower documentation burden. Non-signatories must explain and justify their alternative measures to the AI Office, which is a heavier and less certain exercise.
4. Fine mitigation. Adherence is a relevant factor when the Commission decides on fines (Art. 101 and Art. 99 proportionality factors such as cooperation and mitigation). It is not a defence.

What signing costs and does not do
- It commits you to specific measures. Key examples: documentation retention for 10 years, responding to downstream provider requests within 14 days, copyright opt-out compliance, and, if you have a systemic-risk model, a Safety and Security Framework established within 4 weeks of notification and at least 2 weeks before market placement, Model Reports to the AI Office, red-teaming, and staged serious-incident reporting.
- Signing does not remove obligations. Annex XI/XII documentation, the copyright policy, and the public training-content summary are still required, and the AI Office can still inspect and evaluate.
- It does not cover downstream high-risk or Art. 50 duties for systems you build on top of the model.
- If you fall short of your commitments, you lose the benefit and may face a harder conversation than a non-signatory.

Decision framework
- Are you a GPAI model provider at all? Models trained with at least 10^23 FLOPs are in scope under the Commission guidelines, and the presumption of systemic risk starts at 10^25 FLOPs. If you only use or fine-tune others' models, you are probably a downstream provider or deployer, and the Code is not for you. Check whether a fine-tune makes you a provider under the Commission's guidelines.
- Open-source model? You get a partial exemption (copyright policy and training summary only) unless the model is systemic-risk. You can sign selected chapters, as xAI did, but partial signing leaves you needing alternative evidence for the rest.
- Systemic-risk? The Safety and Security chapter is the hardest part, but building an equivalent framework yourself is costly and harder to justify.
- Legacy model (before 2 Aug 2025)? You have until 2 August 2027, but signing early aligns your programme.
- Do you have a mature alternative, such as ISO 42001 plus your own safety framework? You may still decide not to sign, but plan for AI Office scrutiny and documented equivalence.

Practical steps
1. Gap-assess current practice against the three chapters.
2. Get legal and engineering sign-off on the commitments, especially retention, timelines, and incident reporting.
3. Decide whether to sign all chapters or only some.
4. Assign owners and an audit trail, and prepare for an Art. 91 request regardless of the decision.

This is general compliance information, not legal advice. Review the Code's text and the Commission's current signatory list before deciding.
