Community bank with heavy dependence on one core service provider: late-2026 supervisory developments

Short answer: yes, there are two relevant developments. Neither is final, and the June 2023 Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships (OCC 2023-17) remains the operative framework.

1. What is new
- On September 15, 2026 the OCC, Federal Reserve, FDIC and NCUA published "Proposed Third-Party Risk Management Guidance" in the Federal Register (91 FR 58536).
  - It would rescind and replace the June 2023 guidance. NCUA would join for the first time.
  - It would also rescind the OCC foreign-based third-party bulletin, the 2024 bank-fintech joint statement, and the 2024 community bank TPRM guide.
  - Comments are due November 16, 2026, which is about ten days away.
- The agencies also issued a Joint Statement on Community Banks' Engagement with Core Service Providers. This is the document most directly relevant to your situation. Read it in full and check what it says about your negotiating position, due diligence, and expectations on contracts and oversight. I am not summarising its detailed content because I cannot verify it here.

2. Status and how to treat it
- The proposal is not final. Keep operating to the June 2023 guidance, and the 2024 community bank TPRM guide while it is still in effect.
- Do not rebuild policy around proposed text. Do track it and respond if it affects you. Community bank proportionality and core-provider dependence are the sort of points worth commenting on.

3. Concentration risk: what to do regardless
A single core provider is a classic critical, hard-to-substitute vendor. Treat it as Tier 1 on data access, criticality and substitutability. Regardless of how the proposals land, the following should be in place.
1. Due diligence at the deepest level. Review the SOC 2 Type II or equivalent report. Check the opinion, the period, the system description and scope, and every exception. Extract the complementary user entity controls into an owner-assigned checklist. Review subservice organisations: carve-out versus inclusive method, the complementary subservice organisation controls, and whether the carved-out hosting layer has its own assurance. Request a bridge letter if the period ended more than about three months ago. A bridge letter is a management representation, not auditor assurance.
2. Contract. Seek these clauses: audit and information rights, incident notification SLAs, resilience commitments (RTO/RPO, DR testing), sub-processor change notice, data return and certified deletion, and termination assistance for 90-180 days. Seek fee and renewal protections, and de-conversion assistance. Cores often come with long terms and limited exit rights, so renewal time is your main leverage.
3. Exit and contingency planning. Document a tested exit strategy: data extraction formats, de-conversion timelines, alternative providers and an interim operating mode. Substitutability is the real issue, so be honest about the realistic cost and time to move.
4. Ongoing monitoring. Track financial condition, security incidents, assurance report renewals, SLA performance, fourth-party and sub-processor changes, and integration and API or OAuth scope reviews for every connected fintech.
5. Fourth-party visibility. Identify the core provider's critical subcontractors and hosting providers, and any concentration on the same cloud provider across your vendors.
6. Governance. Board-level reporting on core dependence, an independent programme review, and use of peer and industry groups to push for better terms.

4. Next steps
- Read the Joint Statement and the Federal Register proposal this week.
- Decide whether to submit a comment by November 16, 2026.
- Gap-assess your core provider relationship against the list above, with owners and dates.
- Calendar the contract renewal date and start exit-plan work well before it.

Caveat: this is general compliance information, not legal advice. Confirm details with the primary sources and your examiner or primary regulator.
